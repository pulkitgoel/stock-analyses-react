## 2. What the Company Does

IGI is an independent gemological laboratory. It grades and certifies diamonds, coloured gemstones and finished jewellery, and issues a report that fixes the 4Cs — carat, colour, clarity, cut — that the trade prices against and the consumer relies on. The business is 50 years old, was founded in Antwerp in 1975, is now headquartered in Mumbai, and has been owned by Blackstone since 2023.

Three revenue lines, all fee-based:

- **Lab-grown diamond certification (54% of certification revenue in Q2 FY27).** Loose stones and finished jewellery. IGI was the first laboratory to certify lab-grown diamonds, in 2005, and industry estimates put its share of global lab-grown grading above 70%. This is the growth engine.
- **Natural diamond certification (roughly 30%).** Loose stones and jewellery. A mature, flat-to-slow market where IGI is taking share rather than riding growth.
- **Coloured gemstones and other (6% and rising).** Built around American Gemological Laboratories (AGL), acquired for US$9 million cash and consolidated from January 2026.

The economics are those of a certification monopoly in miniature: revenue is a fee per report (average realisation ₹891 per report in Q2 FY27), the cost base is laboratories and graders, and incremental volume drops through at very high margin. EBITDA margin was 61.4% in Q2 FY27 and has not been below 57% in any of the last twelve quarters. The company is asset-light — capex was ₹81 Cr against ₹624 Cr of operating cash flow in the fifteen months to March 2026.

Where the work physically happens is the structural point. The stones are grown, cut and polished in India, and the grading is done in India; the retail demand that creates the need for a certificate sits in the United States, Europe and the Gulf. India standalone produced ₹3,056 Mn of the ₹3,980 Mn group revenue in Q2 FY27, so three quarters of the revenue and effectively all of the grading capacity is Indian. That is why the company argues the numbers should be read as "one IGI" rather than as an India business with overseas branches.

Why the two failures are artefacts rather than warnings:

- **Durability.** IGI listed in December 2024. The P&L on Screener shows Dec 2022, Dec 2023, Dec 2024 and a 15-month period to March 2026, because the company changed its financial year from calendar to March. There is no five-year series to test, so the gate returns "unknown" and scores it as a fail. The underlying record is 46% sales CAGR from CY2022 to CY2024 and 27% revenue growth in H1 FY27 — but the honest position is that a company with twenty-two months of listed history has not demonstrated durability through a full cycle.
- **Management.** The screener file holds three shareholding columns. The company's own filings show 76.55% in all eight quarters since listing — perfectly flat. The gate's "falling" label is a data gap. A Blackstone-controlled promoter at 76.55% is stable in the sense the gate means, though it carries a different risk that the gate does not test (section 8).

The eighth gate is judgement, and it passes clearly: the **second engine** is not a slide in a deck. AGL contributed ₹244 Mn of certification revenue in Q2 FY27, up from ₹57 Mn a year earlier, taking gemstones from 2% to 6% of revenue; Italy opened this year; Saudi Arabia is being prepared; a Botswana company was incorporated in September 2026. That is new product and new geography with revenue already attached.

**One process finding worth recording.** The radar's momentum score for IGIL was 41.0/100, with the component detail reading "sales -8.8% YoY (0/10), profit -6.3% YoY (0/10)". Those figures are wrong as a description of the business: the scorecard compared TTM revenue of ₹1,457 Cr against the 15-month FY26 column of ₹1,598 Cr. A twelve-month period measured against a fifteen-month period always shows a decline. IGIL's actual latest quarter grew revenue 30.9% and profit 43.1%. Any name in the universe that has just changed its financial year will be under-scored the same way; the scorecard should normalise the last annual column to twelve months before computing growth.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Natural diamond, loose | 490 | 529 | +8% | 14% |

| Other operating income | 96 | 105 | +9% | 3% |
| Certification revenue | 2,941 | 3,875 | +32% | |

Three observations:

1. **The growth is a lab-grown story and the certification fee is holding.** The bear case for a lab-grown grader is that the stone price collapses, the certificate becomes disproportionate to the asset, and fees get competed away. ARP rose 4% year on year. That is the single most important data point against the bear case, and it held in a quarter when lab-grown wholesale prices were stable.
2. **Natural diamond certification is not growing and is 30% of the revenue.** Natural jewellery grew 3% and natural loose 8%, in a market management described as flat. High and volatile gold prices are making natural-diamond buyers slower to commit. IGI is taking share in a shrinking pool, which works for a while and then stops working.
3. **Gemstones is small but is the reason the "second engine" gate passes.** AGL went from ₹57 Mn to ₹244 Mn in four quarters. It is the only line that is not a function of diamond volumes, and management is explicit that AGL's methods are being pushed into the other laboratories and into jewellery clients.

## 7. Cash Flow and Working Capital

H1 FY27: operating cash flow ₹4,157 Mn, 89% of EBITDA and 118% of PAT, helped by a ₹662 Mn working-capital release as receivables fell from a peak of ₹2,885 Mn to ₹2,402 Mn. Cash and bank balance ₹10,749 Mn at September 2026.

Screener's two standing warnings on this stock are worth addressing rather than repeating:

- **"Working capital days have increased from -103 days to 113 days."** True but misleading as a red flag. A certification business collects fees before or on delivery and carries almost no inventory, so working capital days were negative in the IPO year. The move to 113 days is receivables building as the customer mix shifts towards larger growers and retail chains who negotiate credit. The absolute amount is ₹451 Cr on TTM revenue of ₹1,457 Cr. It has stabilised — debtor days 66 at March 2026, and receivables fell 17% in the September half — and cash conversion is 94% of EBITDA. This is a business-model change, not a liquidity problem, but it does mean the days-of-cash cushion is thinner than the headline margin suggests.
- **"Stock is trading at 9.5 to 9.8 times its book value."** Also true, and simply descriptive. A fee business with 69% ROCE and almost no fixed capital will always trade far above book. Book value is not the relevant anchor here; earnings power is.

- **DIIs went 6.38% in March 2026 to 5.47% in September** — 14% of the position sold into the Q1 result. Small in absolute terms.
- **Shareholder count fell 9.9% over two quarters**, from 1,87,343 to 1,68,818, through a period when the stock made new lows. Retail leaving a falling stock is normal; with FII holding roughly flat and public percentage rising, the count is falling in the small-holder tail rather than in size.

**The overhang the shareholding table does not show.** The company listed with 23.45% public float, and SEBI requires 25% minimum public shareholding within three years of listing — December 2027 here. Promoter must come down from 76.55% to at most 75%, which is at minimum 1.55% of equity, about 0.67 Cr shares or ₹267 Cr at the current price. The company's own prospectus lists exactly this as a risk factor: promoter disposal "including to comply with the minimum public shareholding norms". A Blackstone fund that has held since 2023 and is sitting on a position worth ₹13,180 Cr will not stop at the regulatory minimum. The supply event is dated, known, and not in the price.

## 11. Trade Setup

The setup is a breakout that has already run 24% in five sessions. The trade is not "buy the breakout" at ₹398 with RSI at 80; it is "buy the first controlled pullback that holds the breakout level".

- **Entry zone ₹375-385.** That is the top of the old range (₹381.25, the May high) and the 22-23 October congestion. A retest that holds converts the breakout into support.
- **Confirmation wanted:** a session in that zone with delivery above 40% and volume below the 23 October spike. That combination would say the churn was absorbed.
- **Stop loss ₹352**, below the 23 October gap and the 10-day EMA (₹361.56). A close under ₹352 invalidates the breakout and puts ₹330 (the 50 and 200-day averages) back in play.
- **Target 1 ₹419** (the 27 October intraday high), **Target 2 ₹445-450** (a measured move of the ₹291-381 base, projected from the breakout).
- **Risk/reward** from a ₹380 entry with a ₹352 stop and a ₹445 second target is 1:2.3.
- **Invalidation:** any close below ₹352, or a renewed LGD sequential decline in the Q3 print while delivery stays under 35%.

For a longer-horizon position the entry discipline is different and simpler: ₹398 is 25.9x trailing and about 22-23x forward on management's own guidance, which is not a stretched multiple for 27% revenue growth, 69% ROCE and net cash. A first tranche at market with a second on any retest into ₹360-375 is defensible. Chasing a stock at RSI 80 the week after a 20.6%-delivery volume spike is not.

## 14. Bull Case

1. **A certification franchise on the volume side of a structural shift.** Lab-grown loose-stone certification grew 37% and lab-grown jewellery 47% in the latest quarter, with grower capacity still being added. IGI has graded lab-grown since 2005 and industry estimates put its global share above 70%.
2. **The pricing bear case has not shown up in the numbers.** Average realisation per report rose 4% year on year in a quarter when lab-grown prices were flat to falling. The fee is 2-3% of retail, small enough that a retailer will not risk an uncertified stone to save it.
3. **Operating leverage is real and measurable.** Revenue +31%, EBITDA +39%, PAT +43% in Q2. Every incremental rupee of certification revenue lands at roughly 70% margin because the laboratories, the equipment and the graders are already there. Management turned AI scheduling on this year and says the same team is grading more stones.
4. **Balance sheet with options.** Net cash ₹925 Cr, ROCE 69%, CFO/PAT 0.94, capex 5% of revenue. The company can open laboratories (Italy, Saudi Arabia, Botswana), buy capability (AGL for US$9 million) and pay dividends out of cash flow without touching debt.
5. **Guidance was raised, not cut.** The May guidance of 15% revenue and 20% EBITDA growth has been effectively replaced by "similar to H1" — 27% and 34%. For a stock that de-rated on growth doubts through 2025, that is the specific thing that had to change, and it changed.
6. **A second engine that already has revenue.** AGL took gemstones from 2% to 6% of revenue in four quarters, and the same methods are being pushed into IGI's other laboratories.

## 15. Bear Case

1. **One segment. Half the revenue. A deflationary end market.** Fifty-four percent of certification revenue is lab-grown loose stones, and the sequential number was flat (₹2,095 Mn to ₹2,111 Mn). The year-on-year growth is coming from grower capacity running hotter, not from price. If growers stop adding reactors, or if the wholesale price resumes falling, the fee pool stops growing — and the market is paying 25.9x for a business that would then be a 12% grower with a 46% net margin and no second act.
2. **GIA is attacking the price point, and management's answer is assertion.** GIA moved away from full 4Cs reports for lab-grown towards a simplified assessment priced at about US$15 per carat. Asked directly on the call, the CFO said the 4Cs "are not going to go away at all" and that the trade needs them to establish commercial value. That may be right. It is not evidenced in the transcript, and a cheaper accepted certificate is the one thing that breaks a 61% margin.
3. **Grading integrity is the entire franchise and it has been questioned publicly.** Two separate analysts on the latest call raised grading inconsistency and asked whether certificate-printing rights had been given to large Surat growers. Management denied the printing rights and defended the process as ISO-accredited with multiple layers of review. Nothing has been proven. But a laboratory's asset is credibility, and a single grading scandal would be terminal in a way that a bad quarter never is.
4. **The promoter must sell, and will sell more than it must.** 76.55% held by a Blackstone vehicle, with SEBI's 25% minimum public shareholding requirement biting by December 2027 and the company's own prospectus flagging promoter disposal for exactly that reason. At minimum ₹267 Cr of stock has to come to market; a private equity fund eleven years into a hold will clear more than the regulatory floor. The float is ₹4,117 Cr, so this is a real supply event against a thin free float.
5. **A third of revenue is a market that is not growing.** Natural diamond certification was 30% of the segment mix, with natural jewellery at +3% and natural loose at +8% in a flat market. IGI is winning share in a pool that is shrinking as lab-grown takes bridal share; that trade has a floor.
6. **No comparable, no anchor.** There is no listed gemological peer in India and the two global names are private. Every valuation argument here is either against the company's own twenty-two-month history or against a peer set of real estate platforms. That is a genuine information disadvantage, not a formatting problem.
7. **The quarter had a quiet flaw.** Q2 was the best quarter in history on every headline, and the largest segment did not grow sequentially. That is the kind of detail that only shows up when the segment table is read line by line, and it is the reason the stock's next print matters more than the last one.

## 16. Kill Criteria

Written before the analysis, to be judged at the review date rather than argued with hindsight.

- **Primary kill:** lab-grown average realisation per report declines year on year for two consecutive quarters while volumes still grow. That is the signature of certification becoming a commodity and would remove the floor under a 61% EBITDA margin. One quarter is noise; two is a repricing.
- **Secondary kill:** any regulatory or exchange action, class action, or sustained press investigation into grading integrity at IGI's laboratories. Nothing is pending; this is the tail risk that ends the thesis outright.
- **Third:** promoter sell-down materially above the minimum-public-shareholding requirement (more than about 3% of equity) in a single transaction, or a block at a discount to market.
- **Fourth:** natural diamond certification turning negative year on year for two quarters, which would mean the 30% base is shrinking faster than lab-grown can compensate.
- **Thesis-neutral but review-triggering:** the stock closing below ₹352 before it makes a new high, which would invalidate the breakout without invalidating the business.

## 19. Verdict

**WATCH. Quality is not in question; the entry is.**

IGI is the best business the radar has surfaced this month. A 69% ROCE, net cash, 94% cash conversion, a 61% EBITDA margin and 27-31% revenue growth in a franchise that grades more than 70% of the world's lab-grown diamonds is not a statistical artefact — it is a real moat built on having been first in 2005 and on 4.35 million reports a quarter of accumulated grading data. The Q2 result was the best in the company's history on every line, and management raised guidance rather than trimming it, which is the opposite of the pattern that usually precedes a de-rating.

Two things keep it off a buy. First, the price: 25.9x trailing and 22-24x forward is a fair multiple for this quality of business, not a discount, and the stock has already re-rated 26% off its September multiple. Second, the tape: RSI 79.8, above the upper Bollinger band, +24% in five sessions, and the biggest volume day in the stock's history carried 20.6% delivery. The breakout is real, but the marginal buyer on 23 October was a day trader, not an institution.

The business risk that matters is not competition from another laboratory — it is that lab-grown certification becomes a commodity priced per carat rather than per report. The evidence so far says it is not happening: ARP rose 4% year on year. But the largest segment was flat sequentially, GIA is now selling a cheaper simplified grade, and a third of revenue comes from a natural-diamond market that is not growing. That is a good business facing a specific, identifiable structural question, and 25.9x is not the price to pay while the answer is still being written.

**What I would do:** a first tranche now is defensible for a long-horizon holder at this multiple; for a swing position, wait for ₹375-385 with delivery above 40%, stop at ₹352, first target ₹419 and second ₹445-450. Do not chase above ₹410.
