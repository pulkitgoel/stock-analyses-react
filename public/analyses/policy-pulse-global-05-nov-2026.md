## Second: crude's two-way week, and why the second leg matters more

Brent broke **below $100 a barrel for the first time in two months**, having fallen nearly **9-10% in two days** — from $104.77 on Monday, down 7.63% in a single session, through to a low near $93.50.

Then today it bounced. **Brent last traded at $97.06, up 2.58%; WTI $90.21, up 2.37%.** Gold above **$4,117**, up about **5% in two days** — its largest two-day jump since early April. The 10-year Treasury yield sat at **4.87%**, unchanged on the day after yields had fallen hard.

The mechanism for the first leg is now explicit and it is political, not geological. **Trump acknowledged on Wednesday that the war played a role in the Republicans' defeat, and Vice President JD Vance was blunter on X: "The reason we lost is the war."** Traders read that as a president facing a hostile Congress and punished at the pump being **less likely to escalate** with Iran. That is a probability trade on a war, and probability trades in oil are violent in both directions.

The second leg — today's bounce — is the market admitting it does not actually know. Nothing was signed. Hormuz flows have not normalised. **This is a one-way-to-two-way maturity, not a resolution**, and I have flagged that distinction on every run this week because it is the difference between owning energy and renting the position for a week.

Reuters also carried a same-day item that closes the loop: **soaring diesel prices helped usher in the Democratic sweep.** Diesel, not crude, is the price that reaches a trucker and a checkout counter. If the White House's diagnosis is fuel prices, the policy response points at supply and at the war — not at tariffs.

Korea is the AI memory trade in single-market form: **Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix** are the two names that decide that index, and both were sold hard. Japan's weakness concentrated in the same supply chain — Kioxia, Ibiden, Murata, Tokyo Electron.

Two triggers, both about the *financing* of AI rather than the demand for it:

**One, a US political repricing.** Democrats who campaigned on data-centre moratoriums won in **Texas and Georgia**. State Street's Masahiko Loo: the sweep *"raises the political risk premium around US AI without necessarily derailing the broader AI capex cycle."* That is the correct framing and it explains why the losers are data-centre landlords and memory, not software.

**Two, a financing-cost problem that predates the election.** The AI build-out is increasingly debt-funded, and a 10-year at 4.87% with a 30-year above 5.2% makes every incremental gigawatt more expensive to carry. This is the same trade that took the SOX down roughly a quarter in a month before today.

**India's insulation is real but shallow.** The Nifty has almost no memory, no foundry and no hyperscaler capex inside it. What it does have is **IT services**, which rose over 2% today as domestic financials led — the same "reverse AI trade" rotation that showed up in July when Korea crashed. That is a rotation, not a moat: Indian IT's revenue still depends on the same Western clients who are now being asked to justify their AI spend.

## Fourth: the flows, and a rupee that is no longer the story

**FPIs bought ₹13,801.83 crore of Indian equities on 5 November** (buy ₹29,886.70 crore, sell ₹16,084.87 crore, NSE/BSE/MSEI capital-market segment), while **DIIs sold ₹10,107.11 crore.** That is a near-total reversal of the domestic-institutional bid that carried the earlier part of this rally, and it puts foreign money in the driving seat again.

The rupee closed **94.47 per dollar, up 17 paise**, extending gains to a two-week high and now roughly **3% off its record lows**. Softer crude and returning equity flows are doing the work that intervention was doing a month ago.

One honest caveat on the whole six-session move: **this is a rally driven by three external inputs — crude, US yields and FPI flow — none of which is under Indian policy control.** A 2,009-point Sensex day is not evidence that anything domestic improved.

## Fifth: two things from Europe worth a line each

**The Bank of England raised interest rates** — its first increase since 2023 — and signalled more may be necessary if the Iran war continues. That is a G7 central bank tightening *into* an energy shock, which is the opposite of the 2022 playbook, and it is a direct read on how seriously the inflation impulse is being taken.

**Europe's STOXX 600 rose another 0.4%**, leaving it just short of August's record, with banks and drugmakers leading. Within European tech, semiconductor-exposed names fell hard: **ASML, Soitec and Infineon down 3% to 8%.** Renewables rallied — Denmark's **Orsted +5.3%, Vestas +2.3%** — on the same US clean-energy logic as the US session.

## The line to take away

India rose 2.4% because crude fell 10% and foreigners bought ₹13,802 crore. Korea fell 5.3% because the cost of financing AI went up and the politics of AI got worse.

Neither of those is a policy story about India. That is precisely why both are worth watching: the Indian rally is on loan from two external variables, and the AI unwind is the one external variable most likely to call that loan in — through IT services first, and through the rupee second.
