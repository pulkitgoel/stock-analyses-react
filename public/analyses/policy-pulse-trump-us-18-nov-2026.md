The President's account bought and sold Nvidia at least 103 times. On the same day the company stopped shipping the chips it is licensed to sell into China.

Both statements are true this morning, and neither one causes the other. But they describe the same tension, and the disclosure file is now detailed enough to read properly rather than as a headline.

## The file says less than the headline

CNBC's analysis of the annual and periodic filings puts the count at 103 Nvidia transactions since January 2025, worth a combined $27 million to $97.5 million at the disclosed ranges. A Washington Post review estimates the position was worth $13 million to $62 million at the end of August, against $615,000 to $1.3 million at the end of 2024. Nvidia is one of his three largest single holdings.

The trades that draw attention are the ones adjacent to policy. On 18 May the account sold up to $1 million of Nvidia, five days after the Secretary of State reportedly briefed the President on intelligence about a Chinese businesswoman and her relationship with then-chief executive Jensen Huang. On 4 December 2025 the account sold up to $1 million, bought back the next day, and on 8 December the administration announced that Nvidia could sell H200 chips to approved Chinese customers. In August the account bought up to $1.6 million and sold up to $1 million, including a sale of $500,001 to $1 million on 21 August; the disclosure covering it was signed on 17 September and released on 8 October — the day the President awarded Huang the National Medal of Science. An analysis published on 13 November calculates Nvidia fell 22.6% between 21 August and 12 November, having first risen about 11.4% to 6 October.

The scale is the story, not any single trade. He disclosed more than 21,000 trades in 2025 against roughly 500 across his entire first term.

The White House position has not changed and is worth stating plainly: independent third-party institutions manage the portfolio against recognised indexes, including the Schwab 1000, and "the President does not pick or trade individual stocks." The disclosures contain no evidence that he directed a trade or that his advisers saw classified material. A portfolio that tracks a broad index will hold Nvidia, because Nvidia is 5.9% of the S&P 500. Proximity is not causation, and 21,000 trades guarantee that some of them will land near an announcement.

What the file does establish is that the country's chief executive holds a position whose value is set by policy he signs. Congress wrote the conflict-of-interest statute to exclude the President. That is a governance fact, not a trading signal, and readers should treat it as one.

## The revenue line moved the other way

The actionable half of this is on the company's side, and it arrived on the earnings call.

Nvidia's third quarter was a beat on everything: revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year ago and 20% sequentially, against a $109.15 billion consensus; data centre $107.6 billion against $101.85 billion expected; non-GAAP earnings per diluted share of $2.68 against $2.47; gross margin 74.9%. Guidance for the January quarter is $127.4 billion to $132.6 billion against $125.25 billion, and includes an additional week. The company announced a $150 billion buyback and returned $28.1 billion in the quarter.

Then interim chief executive Colette Kress said Nvidia is not currently shipping Hopper-generation data-centre products to China — the most advanced products it is licensed to sell there. It had sold $2.4 billion of them in the previous quarter.

Read those two paragraphs together. The most valuable company in the world is voluntarily forgoing a multi-billion-dollar quarterly revenue line into its largest addressable market, in the same week that the Bureau of Industry and Security closed the remote-access loophole and the President's personal account was reported to have traded the stock 103 times. The personal position is a passive index artefact. The China exit is an active policy choice. Only one of them tells you anything about future cash flows.

## Manila produced four minerals agreements and no text

At the Quad leaders' meeting in Manila, Trump announced what he called several important new critical mineral deals with Australia, India and Japan, aimed at ensuring no member is "vulnerable to coercion by any single foreign supplier." He did not name China. He did say the United States would "catch them within 10 months."

There is no published text. InsideTrade reported that he offered no details on the agreements. The concrete artefacts are institutional rather than commercial: a first Quad Business Leaders' Forum in 2027, joint military exercises, and Quad export credit agencies meeting in Tokyo today. The framework they sit inside was agreed in May 2026 — a $20 billion government-and-private mobilisation target, a Quad Critical Minerals Framework, and a separate India-US framework on critical minerals and rare earths. On 31 October the group announced $10 million for critical minerals recycling.

For US-listed exposure the honest read is that a signing ceremony without tonnage, offtake or a price floor changes sentiment and nothing else. Beijing extended its pause on wider rare-earth controls by two months to 10 January — a truce renewed in two-month increments is a truce in which both sides are buying time. The IEA estimates that if the controls were fully applied, $6.5 trillion a year of downstream production outside China would be at risk. That number is the size of the prize and the reason a communiqué is not a catalyst.

## The Philippines deal Manila does not call a deal

At a state lunch at Malacañang Palace, Trump said the two countries had "consummated a very big trade deal, the biggest trade deal we've ever made so far." On Truth Social he called it "new and completely different," and said it would be "great for our Farmers and those in the business of 'Energy.'"

The Philippine President's office said both sides reached "an understanding on areas of economic cooperation" and did not describe a trade deal, saying only that trade representatives would translate commitments into concrete initiatives. Reuters could not immediately verify the details. Marcos and the US ambassador confirmed at the start of the bilateral that an agreement under Pax Silica — the US-led critical-technologies and minerals supply-chain initiative — would not be signed during the visit.

The number that already exists is the one to anchor to: Philippine exports have carried an additional 12.5% US tariff since 24 July, imposed after Washington found Manila had not effectively enforced a ban on imports made with forced labour. That replaced a 10% levy after the Supreme Court struck down the reciprocal tariff authority; under the earlier regime Manila had 19%. Manila has been negotiating to get back to 10%, which is the rate for countries that have adopted or committed to such bans. A deal that lowers 12.5% to 10% is worth roughly 2.5 points of landed cost. That is the trade. Nothing announced today specifies it.
