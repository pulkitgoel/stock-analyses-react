## 2. Business Overview — What the Company Actually Does

National Aluminium Company is a Navratna central public sector enterprise under the Ministry of Mines, incorporated on 7 January 1981 and listed on the BSE in 1992 and the NSE in 1999. It runs the most integrated bauxite-to-metal chain in India: its own bauxite mine feeds its own refinery, the refinery feeds its own smelter, and the smelter is powered by its own coal-based captive plant and wind farms. Its metal has been registered on the London Metal Exchange since May 1989.

| Asset | Location | Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| Panchpatmali bauxite mine | Koraput, Odisha | 68.25 lakh t/yr |
| Alumina refinery | Damanjodi, Odisha | 21.0 lakh t/yr |
| Aluminium smelter | Angul, Odisha | 4.6 lakh t/yr cast metal |
| Captive power plant | Angul, Odisha | 1,200 MW (10 x 120); 810 MW to smelter, 30 MW to grid |
| Utkal D & E coal mine | Angul, Odisha | 40 lakh t/yr |
| Wind power | Odisha | 198 MW |
| Port facilities | Visakhapatnam | Alumina export 11.8 lakh t/yr |

Capacities and the split between internal alumina consumption (9.2 lakh t) and export (11.8 lakh t) are from the company's investor presentation of 14 September 2026.

**How it makes money.** Two commodity streams and one internal cost centre. Alumina is sold both internally to the smelter and externally to third parties, largely on spot tenders to the Middle East; aluminium metal is sold domestically at an LME-linked price plus duty, a premium and freight equalisation. Power is not a profit centre — it is the reason NALCO's cost position is defensible. The company describes itself, citing a Wood Mackenzie report, as the lowest-cost producer of bauxite and alumina in the world; that is the company's own claim rather than an independently verified fact, and it is the claim the whole investment case rests on.

### 3.4 Primary trigger, revisited — the LME crash was real and it never reversed

**The original finding holds.** NALCO's earnings are a leveraged bet on the aluminium price, and the aluminium price broke in June 2026. What the first version of this note could not know is that the break was not a two-week event. Four months later the metal is lower still.

Three facts sit in that table and they pull in opposite directions:

- **The metal is 21.1% below its 2 June peak.** That is the main explanation for the share price, though not the whole one: NALCO has fallen **32.2%** from a high of ₹445.15 set on **27 April** — six weeks *before* the metal topped — so the stock led the commodity down and has overshot it by roughly half again. That is what operating leverage does in both directions.
- **Visible supply is at the lowest level in the exchange's modern record.** LME stocks have gone from 509,250 tonnes on 2 January to 235,800 tonnes on 23 October, a fall of **53.7%**. That is not a market drowning in metal.
- **Yet the price keeps sliding.** The market has decided that Chinese alloy exports and Indonesian primary metal will cover the roughly 3 million tonnes of Gulf capacity knocked out during the Middle East conflict. That judgement may be wrong; it is currently what the price says.

**The sequencing matters, and the original note got it backwards.** On 27 April, with LME cash still at $3,662, the stock peaked at ₹445.15. By 12 June it had already lost **15.3%** to ₹376.85 while the metal was still above $3,500. The share price began discounting an aluminium decline *before* the aluminium decline happened. What the 15 June LME break did was confirm a de-rating that was six weeks old, not start one — which is why a single-day "root cause" framing was directionally right and temporally late. It also means the stock's weakness since is not a reaction to the metal; it is the same discounting process continuing as the metal catches up.

What the company is actually paid for its alumina, as distinct from the LME metal number, is management's guidance rather than the exchange series, and it is set out in section 8.4.

## 7. Order Book and Revenue Visibility

### 7.1 What stands in for an order book

**NALCO has no order book, and none should be expected of it.** It is a commodity producer selling spot tonnes, not a capital-goods company with a contracted backlog. Any NALCO write-up that quotes an order book is either confused or describing a different company. What replaces it is volume visibility, and on that measure the company has been unusually explicit:

| Driver | Guidance or fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Alumina sales FY27 | 16 lakh t, against 14 lakh t in FY26 | Q1 call, 3 Aug 2026 |
| Alumina production FY27 | 25 lakh t, of which 2 lakh t from the 5th Stream | Q1 call |
| Alumina realisation Q2 FY27 | ~$370/t, against $323/t in Q1 | Q1 call |
| Aluminium metal premium | ~$110/t in the last export tender, against ~$60 previously | Q1 call |
| Metal cost of production Q2 FY27 | ₹1,70,000–1,72,000/t, against ~₹1,70,000 in Q1 | Q1 call |
| Captive coal FY27 | 4.8 mt, against 4.0 mt in FY26 | Q1 call |
| Smelter capacity use | 5–10% above rated capacity targeted for the year | Q1 call |

Three structural features do the work an order book does elsewhere. **Captive power** removes the single largest and most volatile input cost — 810 MW of the 1,200 MW plant is dedicated to the smelter. **Captive coal** at ₹1,600/t against ₹3,500–3,600/t for e-auction coal is a ₹1,500/t cost gap that widens as the 4.8 mt target is met, and the environmental clearance for the expansion was still pending at the last update. And **the alumina contract structure** — spot tenders, price fixed per shipment, 60–70% of the alumina going to the Middle East — means NALCO takes the spot price rather than a hedged one. That is where the volatility enters.

The honest summary: volumes are visible and guided, prices are not. A reader should treat the volume rows above as reasonably firm and the price rows as management's read on a market that has moved against them since.

### 7.2 The pipeline — what is actually being built

This is the part of the NALCO story that the share price is not paying for, and also the part where the company's own documents have slipped once already. Both are true.

| Project | Capacity | Capex | Status as of the latest filing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5th Stream refinery, Damanjodi | 1.0 MTPA (2.1 → 3.1 MTPA) | ₹5,600 Cr, internal accrual | Pre-commissioning from June 2026; mechanical completion guided for Sept-end; production guided Nov–Dec 2026; 2 lakh t in FY27 only |
| Pottangi bauxite mine | 3.5 MTPA (111 mt reserve) | MDO-operated | MDO appointed Dec 2025; approach road pending on local agitation as of 14 Sep 2026 |
| Aluminium smelter, Angul | 0.5 MTPA (4.6 → ~9.6 lakh TPA) | ₹17,000–18,000 Cr | EGA DX+ Ultra licence **signed** 7 Sep 2026; DPR Board approval guided Oct–Nov 2026 |
| Captive power plant, Angul | 1,080 MW (4×270) | ₹6,000 Cr from NALCO | 50:50 JV with NLC; DPR under process |
| Captive coal, Utkal D & E | 4.8 mt FY27 vs 4.0 mt FY26 | — | EC expected within 2–3 months, per 3 Aug call |

Three things about this table matter more than the numbers.

**The 5th Stream is a FY28 story, not a FY27 one.** One million tonnes of capacity, of which 0.2 million tonnes lands this year. That 0.2 million tonne figure, incidentally, is exactly the same as the alumina sales volume increase management guided for (16 lakh t from 14 lakh t) — the two are the same 2 lakh tonnes, sold rather than stockpiled.

**The coal mine is the underappreciated piece.** Captive coal lands at about ₹1,600/t against ₹3,500–3,600/t for e-auction coal — a ₹1,500/t gap. NALCO ran 4.0 mt of captive coal in FY26 and is targeting 4.8 mt in FY27, with the environmental clearance for the expansion still pending. Every million tonnes shifted from auction to captive is roughly ₹150 crore of annual cost, and it is the only lever that works against caustic soda, CP coke and HFO going the other way.

**The ₹24,000 crore number is NALCO's share, not the project's.** The power plant is ₹12,000 crore in a 50:50 JV with NLC, with the JV borrowing 70%. NALCO's own contribution across smelter and power to 2030-31 is about ₹24,000 crore, and it says it will fund all of it from internal accruals off a ₹10,500 crore cash pile that grows ₹3,500 crore a year after dividends. That is credible on current earnings and would become strained on a prolonged metal-price fall — which is precisely the risk the stock is discounting.

Volume guidance converted. Record Q1 bauxite excavation, calcined alumina and alumina/hydrate sales; four refinery lines running at their 2.1 MTPA rating with a stated target of 5–10% above rated capacity for the year. The EGA technology licence, described on the call as "going to sign this month", was signed on 7 September. That is the one forward-looking promise in the entire set that arrived on time and in the month it was promised.

### 8.2 What slipped

The 5th Stream refinery's mechanical completion, originally guided for June–July, was pushed to **September-end**, with actual production from **November–December** and only **2 lakh tonnes** of the 10 lakh tonne capacity delivered in FY27. The 0.5 MTPA smelter DPR was guided for Board approval in **October–November 2026**, packages ordered by August–September 2027, ground-breaking October–November 2027, completion by **December 2030**. Total capex to 2030-31 was put at **₹24,000 crore** for NALCO's share (₹17,000–18,000 Cr smelter, ₹6,000 Cr power contribution), with FY27 capex of ₹1,500–1,800 Cr stepping to ₹2,500 Cr in FY28, funded from net cash of **₹10,500+ crore** and zero debt.

The slippage is not an inference. Two weeks after the September-end target was set on the call, the company's own investor presentation of 14 September still read "5th Stream: Pre-commissioning started from June'26" with no mechanical completion date, and the same deck still had "Pottangi: Approach Road work — Pending (Local agitation)" (section 18, items 2 and 5). Management described the slippage as "2, 3 months"; by the company's own documents, mechanical completion had not been reached.

### 8.3 The cost side, quantified

This is the part of the call that the June note did not have and that matters most for the 2 November print:

- Metal cost of production rose from about ₹1,57,000/t in FY26 to about ₹1,70,000/t in Q1 FY27 and was guided to ₹1,70,000–1,72,000/t in Q2 — driven by caustic soda (₹45,000/t in Q1, guided ~₹49,000/t in Q2), CP coke (₹44,000/t last year to ₹66,000–70,000/t) and HFO (₹46,000/t to ₹75,000/t).
- Alumina cost of production was ₹22,766/t in Q1 against roughly ₹20,000/t the prior year, on the same inputs.
- Employee cost is falling, not rising: average CTC ₹33 lakh in Q1 against ₹36 lakh in FY26, with headcount at 4,848. Management expects 170–200 retirements a year for the next three to four years. The offset is the 1 January 2027 pay revision, which adds roughly 15% to the wage bill in Q4 FY27.

### 8.4 Realisations, and the Q2 arithmetic

**What NALCO actually gets paid, versus the LME number.** The two are not the same, and this is the single most misread part of the story. The company exports alumina on spot tenders at a fixed price per shipment, and alumina is not LME-linked. On the 3 August call, management guided Q2 FY27 alumina realisation to about **$370/t** against a Q1 average of **$323/t** — a 14.6% improvement while the metal was falling 9.7% (Q2 LME average $3,226 against Q1's $3,571). Domestic metal realisations additionally carry a premium, which the company tendered at about **$110/t** in Q1 against roughly $60/t at the previous tender.

On the company's own 1.6 million tonne alumina sales guidance and ₹94.93/USD, a sustained $47/t improvement is worth roughly **₹700 crore of additional annual revenue**. That is our arithmetic from the company's numbers, not company guidance, and it is the reason the Q2 result on 2 November is genuinely open rather than pre-written.

### 8.5 What the commentary is worth

The call was candid where candour costs something: the 5th Stream dates were revised in public rather than slipped quietly, the input-cost inflation was given line by line with numbers rather than adjectives, and the alumina realisation guidance was specific enough to be checked against the print. The place to be sceptical is the metal price. Management's own view on 3 August was $3,000–3,200/t for the rest of FY27; the metal is $3,043 today, at the bottom of that range and below the Q1 average the record quarter was earned on. The company told the truth about its costs and was, so far, roughly right about the metal — which is a better record than most commodity producers manage, and still not an argument for buying the stock before the base forms.

### 11.4 Two cross-checks, and what each is worth

**The 0.14 PEG is an artefact.** Screener reports a PEG of 0.14, computed as the trailing P/E of 8.21 divided by a three-year profit growth of 59.3%. That growth rate is measured off FY23's ₹1,435 crore — the 16% operating-margin trough, when the same assets that earned ₹5,797 crore in FY26 earned a quarter of it. The arithmetic is (5,797 ÷ 1,435)^(1/3) − 1 = 59.3%. Dividing a trough multiple by a recovery rate produces a number that describes the cycle, not the value. The same objection, in milder form, applies to the 34.9% five-year profit CAGR that Screener lists as a virtue in section 14: (5,797 ÷ 1,299)^(1/5) − 1 = 34.9%, measured from FY21's ₹1,299 crore. Anyone quoting "PEG 0.14, deeply undervalued" is reading a base-year effect.

**The Graham number is a sanity check that happens to land close.** √(22.5 × 36.77 × 118) = ₹312. Against ₹301.75 the stock sits 3.4% below it. The formula caps the multiple at 15x by construction and comes from an era before commodity cycles were priced continuously, so it settles nothing; but it is built from the same two numbers the market is looking at, and the fact that a 15x-capped rule of thumb lands within ₹10 of the price says the market is not paying for growth at all.

### 11.5 The honest counter

Trailing earnings are the wrong denominator for a commodity producer near a price peak in Q1 and falling since. At the Q1 annualised EPS of ₹43.64 the stock is on 6.9x *if* Q1 margins repeat — and Q2's LME average is 9.7% below Q1's. Alumina at $370 against $323 offsets some of it; nobody outside the company knows how much. **The 2 November result is the first real read on that arithmetic, and the stock will move on it.**

## 13. Key Risks

**1. The aluminium price is the earnings, and it is still falling.** LME cash is $3,043/t against $3,855/t on 2 June. The stock has fallen 32.2% against the metal's 21.1%, so the market is already applying roughly 1.5x operating leverage — and it will keep applying it if the metal goes to $2,800. Management's own view on the 3 August call was $3,000–3,200 for the rest of FY27, which is roughly where the metal now is. There is no company-specific lever against this.

**2. Project slippage, already demonstrated once.** The 5th Stream's mechanical completion moved from June–July to September-end and production from mid-year to November–December, a slippage management described as "2, 3 months". The 14 September investor presentation — two weeks after the new September-end target — still showed the project at "pre-commissioning started". A second slip pushes the 3.1 MTPA capacity story into FY28 entirely and removes the volume growth the FY27 guidance assumes.

**3. Input cost inflation, quantified and running against them.** Metal cost of production rose roughly ₹13,000/t year on year to ~₹1,70,000/t: caustic soda ₹45,000 → ~₹49,000/t, CP coke ₹44,000 → ₹66,000–70,000/t, HFO ₹46,000 → ₹75,000/t. Alumina cost of production went from ~₹20,000/t to ₹22,766/t. The offset is alumina realisations at $370 rather than $323, and that offset is a market price, not a contract.

**4. Board composition, and a live exchange penalty.** BSE and NSE fined the company ₹14,13,340 each for non-compliance with six SEBI LODR provisions on board and committee composition for the quarter ended 30 June 2026. NALCO's defence — that a CPSE's directors are appointed by the President of India — is accurate and is also the reason the problem may recur. A company that cannot constitute its audit and nomination committees within the regulations carries a governance discount, however small.

**5. Bauxite dependence on a single ageing mine.** Panchpatmali holds about 110 million tonnes of reserve with an estimated 15–20 year life at current run-rates. Pottangi, the 3.5 MTPA replacement, had its approach road still "pending (local agitation)" in the company's own September presentation. Bauxite is the reason NALCO is a low-cost producer; a disruption at one mine is a disruption to the entire chain.

**6. Coal logistics.** Refinery coal stock was 2–3 days against a normal 10–15 days on the 3 August call, because rail rakes are prioritised to power plants. The smelter's power plant is covered by captive coal, so this is a refinery risk rather than a smelter one — but it is a real one, and it is outside the company's control.

**7. The wage bill turns in Q4 FY27.** Headcount is falling 170–200 a year and average CTC dropped from ₹36 lakh to ₹33 lakh, which has been flattering the cost line. The 1 January 2027 pay revision adds roughly 15% to the wage bill in Q4. The tailwind becomes a headwind in the last quarter of the year.

**8. Funding ₹24,000 crore from internal accruals assumes the accruals.** The plan is credible at ₹3,500 crore of annual cash accretion after dividends, and it becomes strained on a prolonged price fall — precisely when the capex is heaviest, in FY29 and FY30. A CPSE in that position raises debt or cuts the dividend, and neither is good for this shareholder base.

**9. Foreign ownership at 21.98% is both the support and the risk.** FIIs added 5.8 percentage points over four quarters and have held through a 32% drawdown. If a global emerging-market allocation turns, that stake is the most liquid thing to sell in the name.
