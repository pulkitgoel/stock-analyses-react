The vote changed who can stop whom. It did not change a single duty rate. And by Thursday morning the market had stopped trading the first thing and started trading the second.

That is the whole of today. The rally that greeted the result lasted one session. What replaced it is a much narrower, more mechanical trade: the parts of the market that had priced a policy reversal are giving it back, because the arithmetic never supported one.

## First: the number that makes the election almost irrelevant to tariffs

Democrats won both chambers. Reuters' projection puts them at **at least 235 House seats** and **54 Senate seats**, with Mississippi and Alaska outstanding.

Here is what that buys them, in the only currency that matters for a tariff: **a veto override needs 67 senators and 290 House members.** They have neither and are not close to either. Even the 60-vote cloture threshold for ordinary legislation requires Republican votes.

So the legislative path to undoing a tariff is closed. What opens instead is oversight — committee gavels, subpoenas, hearings on how exemptions were granted and which companies lobbied for them. Trade lawyers spent Wednesday telling clients exactly that, in blunter language than usual:

> "The swing in Congress, the results of the election, are very unlikely to have a significant impact on the president's trade agenda and the tariffs that are in place. This is because there is so much trade authority within the executive branch that's been delegated by Congress."
> — **Blake Harden**, managing director for trade policy, Ernst & Young Washington Council

The laws Democrats most want to rescind are the archaic ones — **Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930**, used for the first time in roughly a century against Canadian imports, is the named target. They can pass such a bill in the House. They cannot make it law.

**And the executive is not standing still.** The **Section 301 unfair-trade-practices probe into structural excess industrial capacity** is live and explicitly covers **China, India, Vietnam and more than a dozen other partners**. That investigation is the mechanism by which new duties arrive without a vote. Nothing about Tuesday's result slows it. A former White House trade adviser put the posture plainly: *"I don't think he's going to let his foot off the gas. He's in legacy-building mode right now."*

Trump's own framing, within hours of the result, was of a man who intends to use the veto and then negotiate from it — legislation that is *"insane"* gets vetoed, and after a few months of vetoes *"we'll work together."* He also acknowledged the war cost him votes; Vice President **JD Vance** was more direct on X: *"The reason we lost is the war."*

That admission is more trade-relevant than it looks. If the White House's own diagnosis of the defeat is the price at the pump, the policy response points at **energy prices**, not at the tariff schedule. It is also why crude's move this week is the single most important macro input in the complex — see the Global note.

Three separate causes stacked into one session, and they should be kept apart:

**One, the political risk premium on AI infrastructure repriced.** Democrats who campaigned on data-centre moratoriums won in **Texas and Georgia**. State Street's Masahiko Loo framed it precisely: the sweep *"raises the political risk premium around US AI without necessarily derailing the broader AI capex cycle."* That is the correct read, and it is why the data-centre landlords — IREN, CORZ and peers — have been the most violently repriced names in the market all week rather than the chip designers.

**Two, Qualcomm is a company problem, not a policy problem.** Its handset guidance is weak. A 10% move on that is an earnings event wearing a macro costume.

**Three, memory is the epicentre.** Micron at $698 is a long way from the $900 line it defended in October. The memory complex is where the AI capex trade is most leveraged and least diversified: every hyperscaler spending decision lands there first.

## Fourth: the one dated tariff event nobody is trading yet

Buried under the election coverage is a scheduled policy change with a hard date and a known magnitude.

**US Section 232 duties on Canadian kitchen cabinets double from 25% to 50% on 1 January 2027.** The same increase applies to larger sectors, **including autos**. A Honda executive has already called it *"catastrophic."*

The industry is small enough to be ignored and large enough to matter locally: thousands of fragmented, labour-intensive manufacturers employing close to 25,000 people, which have already shed more than 10% of their workforce since the duties began. One Quebec maker, Cabico, has seen US shipment volumes fall 25% from 2024 and has cut more than a quarter of 700 jobs.

Canada's response has been a **provisional 25% surtax** on certain wood cabinets and vanities, for up to 200 days, while the Canadian International Trade Tribunal investigates. The inquiry ends **15 January**. Manufacturers say the surtax has not stemmed imports and want 100%.

Talks between Ottawa and Washington collapsed in August and then resumed before the midterms, because the tariffs were unpopular in several battleground states. Canada's trade minister says the election *"would not change Ottawa's stance."*

The investment read is not "buy or sell Canada." It is that **1 January is a live, dated cost event for any US retailer or homebuilder with Canadian cabinet, vanity or auto exposure** — and it is the clearest example of the pattern that survives this election: schedules already signed keep running on their own clock.

## The line to take away

An election that cannot change a tariff is not a trade event. It is an oversight event. The market figured that out in one session, and the money that had been parked in the reversal trade moved into cash-flow sectors and out of capex.

What actually moves US equities from here is the same thing that moved them this week: whether hyperscalers keep spending, and whether the war ends. Neither is on the ballot, and neither is in Congress's hands.
