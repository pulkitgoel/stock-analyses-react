Democrats won the Congress. They did not win the tariff schedule.

That gap is the entire investment question, and the market answered it in ninety minutes. The Dow ran up 1,203 points by 09:46 a.m. ET. Solar and health insurers led. Defence contractors and AI data-centre landlords went the other way. Nothing in the vote itself changed a single duty rate — but it changed who can stop whom for the next two years.

## First: the result, and the number that matters

**Democrats have won control of both the Senate and the House.** The Senate count settled near **54-44** with two seats still to be called; the House sat around **228-139** with the remainder uncalled. Seven Senate seats changed hands. Texas, Kansas and South Carolina elected Democratic senators for the first time in decades. In Texas, Democrat **Gina Hinojosa** beat Governor **Greg Abbott** — and she campaigned on an immediate pause on data centres, which is why that sector moved before the opening bell.

Here is the arithmetic that decides what is actually legislable. A veto override needs **two-thirds in both chambers**. Democrats are nowhere near it. **Trump said it himself, on Fox News, within hours of the result:**

> "They'll send over things that are insane and I'll veto them. And after a couple of months of vetoes, they'll go out and they'll say, 'How do we get this stuff done?' And we'll work together."

He posted an image of a red hat reading **"Veto"** on Truth Social late Tuesday. He also told the Wall Street Journal he expects to work with the new majority and get along well with them. Both things are true at once, and both are consistent with the market's read: **gridlock, not reversal.**

What Congress *can* do without sixty votes is investigate, subpoena, refuse to confirm, and — critically — **refuse to appropriate.** The current funding measure runs out on **11 December**. That is the first real lever, and it now sits with the opposition. It is also why the defence complex sold off.

## Second: the tape, stated as intraday and not as a close

The US session is still open as this is written. Tuesday's close is the last settled print and it is the correct base:

**Tuesday 3 November close:** S&P 500 **7,568.92 (+0.36%)**, Nasdaq Composite **25,372.01 (+0.83%)**, Dow **51,787.04 (-0.38%)**. The Nasdaq-100 had confirmed a technical correction on Monday.

**Wednesday, 09:46 a.m. ET:** Dow **52,989.79 (+1,203.07, +2.30%)**, S&P 500 **7,698.80 (+129.88, +1.72%)**, Nasdaq Composite **25,912.39 (+540.38, +2.13%)**. Ten-year Treasury yield eased to about **4.92%** from 4.95%. The dollar weakened against most currencies. Advancers led decliners 3.6-to-1 on the NYSE.

The overnight path is worth recording because it shows how thin the conviction was. Nasdaq-100 futures hit **27,615.75**, their lowest since 30 July, as traders priced a Democratic sweep. They were back at **28,545.50** by 6:25 a.m. — a **3.4%** round trip — after Trump accepted the result. Futures initially fell, then reversed. That is not a market that knows what the election means. It is a market that knows what it *feared* and stopped fearing it.

## Third: the dispersion is the story, and it is not about the election

The index move is noise. The single-stock moves describe a specific set of policy expectations that the vote made more likely, and each one can be checked against a mechanism.

**Clean energy repriced hardest.** **First Solar +15.1%**, **Enphase +12.1%**, **Array Technologies +21.8%**, and the Invesco Solar ETF **+8.3%**. The mechanism is straightforward: federal support for clean-energy development is now less likely to be clawed back, because the chamber that would have to pass a clawback is controlled by the party that opposes it. Bloomberg reported the rally was global, not just US.

**Mega-cap tech caught up after lagging.** **Amazon +7.1%**, **Alphabet +3.4%**, **Meta +1.6%** by mid-morning, with Microsoft and Apple up 6-7% on some prints. The read here is *less* legislative risk to large platforms — a Congress that cannot pass a sweeping tech bill cannot pass a hostile one either.

**AI infrastructure was the loser, and this is the trade that survived the election.** Data-centre operators **IREN, Hut 8, Applied Digital and Core Scientific fell between 1.2% and 13%.** Citi's US equity strategy head **Scott Chronert**: *"It's pretty clear that the AI trade will face some headwinds there.... If you push out the pace or change the pace of the data center build out, you begin to change the fundamental trajectory for many of those suppliers."* Democrats made gains in Texas and Georgia, where local opposition to data-centre expansion has been building and where ballot measures went against the industry. **Nvidia fell 1.5%** and was the heaviest single weight on the S&P 500.

**Defence broke down.** **Northrop Grumman, RTX and Lockheed Martin fell sharply** because Trump's **$1.5 trillion** budget request now faces a Congress that did not write it. This is the cleanest causal link on the board: a budget request is a legislative act, and the legislature changed hands.

**The Musk complex fell.** **Tesla -3.9%**, SpaceX down 1.9%. Musk spent close to half a billion dollars backing Republican candidates including the Texas effort.

**DJT fell 10%** on the House projection. **CVS announced a break-up** into pharmacy and insurance businesses and its shares fell. Health insurers and hospital operators rallied broadly.

## Fourth: tariffs — the authority did not move

This is where the coverage and the cash flows diverge, so state it plainly. **Nothing about the election removed a single tariff.** The Section 232 and Section 301 authorities, the Treasury sanctions machinery and USTR's investigative power are all executive. Democrats would need two-thirds in both chambers to override a veto of any reversal bill.

Yesterday's Goldman Sachs note — **Alec Phillips and Abhay Duggirala** — remains the operative forecast, and the election result does not contradict it: a little **under 2 percentage points added to the US effective tariff rate** after the vote, as narrow reversals (Canada, a few products) are outweighed by broader increases.

Two named risks sit on either side of that. **Bernd Lange**, who chairs the European Parliament's trade committee: *"A weakened Trump could become even more aggressive and unpredictable. A lame duck could quickly turn into an angry duck."* And ING's **Carsten Brzeski** made the same point about NATO spending and tariffs. Against that, the counter-evidence from last week still stands: **on 28 October Trump declared imported robotics a national security risk and then imposed no tariffs.** A president who declines a ready-made action is a president whose next action is a choice.

## Fifth: leader trades — nothing new, and saying so

**There was no new presidential financial disclosure today.** The most recent remains the August-period filing released **8 October**: **517 transactions**, purchases of at least **$44.2 million** and sales of at least **$30.1 million**, including up to **$25 million** of Meta and up to **$5 million** of SpaceX debt — the latter bought **two days before the 20 August space-transportation policy memorandum**.

That sequencing is the only genuinely interesting thing in the filing, and it is three weeks old. It is being noted rather than dressed up as today's news. Worth adding: the SpaceX position did not help today — the name fell 1.9% — which is a useful reminder that a disclosure tells you what was bought, not what happens next.

## What to watch

**11 December** is now the date that matters most, because it is the first time the new majority can refuse money. **The 20 January inauguration of the new Congress** is when subpoena power transfers. And the live question for the tariff complex is narrower than the headlines suggest: whether the excess-capacity investigation names product categories, which is what would convert Goldman's two percentage points from a forecast into a duty.

One more thing worth flagging. Trump told Fox the loss was about *"the gasoline price"* rather than the war — *"people agree with what I'm doing on Iran, but they really probably weren't willing to do the short-term consequence of paying more for gasoline."* If that is the diagnosis the White House is working from, the policy response is energy prices, not trade. That would be a different set of winners than the ones that rallied today.
