The election changed who Trump has to persuade. It did not change what he can do without persuading anyone.

That distinction is doing all the work in the global read today, and it is why the biggest moves were in clean energy — a sector whose economics depend on legislation — and the smallest were in tariffs, where the authority is executive.

## United States: the clean-energy repricing was global, not American

Bloomberg reported renewable stocks rallying **around the world**, not just in New York, on the Democratic sweep. The US names led:

**First Solar +15.1%. Enphase Energy +12.1%. Array Technologies +21.8%.** The Invesco Solar ETF gained **8.3%**.

The mechanism is legislative. Federal support for clean-energy development survives because the chamber that would have to vote to remove it is now controlled by the party that opposes removal. For a sector whose project economics are a function of tax credits with multi-year horizons, that is not a sentiment change — it is a discount-rate change on every pipeline in the book.

Array's move has a second component worth separating: it also announced a combination with **Shoals Technologies**. A 21.8% move on a merger announcement is not a clean read on the election.

## India: the relief is real but it is about leverage, not rules

Indian officials and trade experts converged on the same qualified conclusion today, and the qualification is the point.

An official source, quoted by The Hindu BusinessLine: *"Trump has been significantly weakened. Earlier, he had the backing of the House of Representatives, the Senate as well as his own Presidency. Now, he may only have his Presidency left. His attitude towards trading partners, including India, is expected to soften somewhat as he would be more interested in winning friends. There will be less pressure on countries like India to do his bidding."*

Trade expert **Biswajit Dhar** made the institutional version of the same argument: Congressional committees can now scrutinise the economic impact of tariffs and use budgetary powers to extract concessions, so Trump *"will not have that kind of control."*

Against that, three counterweights that a reader should hold simultaneously:

**The authorities are untouched.** **Ajay Srivastava** of GTRI: *"Trump could continue using existing executive authorities, including Sections 232 and 301, along with Treasury sanctions and USTR measures, to exert trade pressure without fresh Congressional approval."* He adds the inversion most people will miss — with his leverage against Russia, China and Iran constrained, Trump *"may also seek a quick trade deal with India to demonstrate American influence."* A weaker president can be a faster dealmaker, not a slower one.

**Bipartisan protectionism.** Srivastava's warning is that both parties now support domestic manufacturing and protective tariffs. The Biden administration retained most Trump-era trade positions including the WTO Appellate Body blockade. A change of majority is not a change of direction.

**Former Ambassador Anil Trigunayat** framed the Indian interest precisely: the hope is that the political setback constrains measures affecting **energy security**, including the threat of sanctions over Russian crude purchases. That threat runs through Treasury and the Graham Act process, both executive.

Reuters adds the diplomatic read: allies with large investment pledges — **Japan at $550 billion, South Korea at $350 billion** — may now feel less pressure to deliver. **Heo Yoon** of Sogang University: *"Democrats seem to perceive that the investment commitments were forced on allies."* And **Rajiv Bhatia** of Gateway House, on India specifically: *"A Democratic victory will not necessarily make Trump more accommodating towards any country."*

On the deal itself: India's trade minister said earlier this month that the **first phase of the India-US trade deal was in the final stages of drafting**, with New Delhi insisting on a preferential edge over Vietnam, Bangladesh and Thailand. Reuters reports the midterm result may now push India toward a wait-and-see posture — which is a negotiating tactic, not a policy change.

## India: IT was the only sector that fell, and the reason is contested

**Nifty IT -1.50% to 32,061.90.** **TCS -2.75% to ₹2,430.40. Infosys -2.33% to ₹1,121.30. HCL Technologies -1.87%.** Every other sector rose, the Nifty gained 0.63%, and IT fell anyway.

**Sunny Agrawal** of SBICAPS Securities gave the standard explanation: the selling is *"likely some caution due to the changing political landscape in the US, their key market."*

There is a credible counter, and it is worth publishing rather than smoothing away. Analysts quoted by Deccan Herald argue the outcome is **unlikely to materially hit Indian IT because the industry's direct H-1B dependence has fallen** — the visa channel that made US politics a first-order earnings variable a decade ago is no longer the same share of cost or of delivery.

Both can be true. The move may be **sentiment** on a day when flows rotated into domestic cyclicals, rather than a repricing of earnings. The tell is that the fall was concentrated in the large-caps with the biggest US revenue share and no corresponding move in mid-cap IT — which is what a de-risking looks like, not what a demand cut looks like.

## Commodities: cheaper crude is a transfer, and India is the recipient

Crude continued to ease, with **Brent trading below $102 and WTI near $94**, extending a fall that began when Trump said Tehran wanted *"to make a deal"* and Vice President Vance told CNN he felt *"really good"* about a negotiated settlement. Brent settled Monday at **$104.77**, down 7.63%.

The rupee strengthened a fourth session to **94.64** against the dollar on a softer dollar index and weaker crude. For India — importing roughly 85% of its crude — a lower flat price and a stronger rupee compound in the same direction on the import bill.

**Global: the AI infrastructure trade is the one that broke**

The AI build-out was the loser on a day the Dow rose 2.3%. Data-centre operators **IREN, Hut 8, Applied Digital and Core Scientific fell between 1.2% and 13%** as Democratic gains in Texas and Georgia put local permitting and ballot opposition in play. **Nvidia fell 1.5%** and was the heaviest weight on the S&P 500. Citi's **Scott Chronert**: *"If you push out the pace or change the pace of the data center build out, you begin to change the fundamental trajectory for many of those suppliers."*

This is the structural signal to carry forward, and it is not an election story. It is the first time the AI infrastructure trade has been repriced by **local political risk** rather than by capex disappointment — and local politics does not resolve in one quarter.

## What to watch

**The India-US first-phase draft.** Whether it is signed in the next six weeks is now the single largest swing factor for Indian export-facing equities, and the election makes both outcomes more likely — a quick deal to demonstrate influence, or a frozen one while Washington reorganises.

**Whether the Graham Act process produces a target list.** The Russian-crude secondary-sanction threat is executive, not legislative, and the political weakening of the president does not remove it. India's exposure attaches only to purchases knowingly made on or after the operative date.

**Brent at $100.** Two 3-7% down days have taken roughly a round trip off a October gain of more than 10%. One more leg holds and the Indian import-bill trade has room; a reversal on any Iran escalation unwinds it in a session.
