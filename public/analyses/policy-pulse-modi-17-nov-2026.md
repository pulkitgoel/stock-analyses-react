Narendra Modi's Manila trip produced a list of proposals and no signature, which is the correct way to read it.

At the 23rd ASEAN-India Summit on Tuesday the Prime Minister put forward a long-term maritime cooperation plan covering connectivity, research, disaster management and maritime security; a joint cyber forensics training programme aimed at cross-border fraud and online scams; ASEAN-India nuclear energy technical exchanges; an ASEAN-India space hackathon for agriculture and resource management; a joint mechanism on mining and critical minerals; a youth summit, young-parliamentarians exchange and startup hackathon; and an ASEAN-India creative partnership pavilion at WAVES.

According to DW, he also proposed free e-visas for tourists from ASEAN countries.

None of those is an order, a notification or a signed instrument. They are proposals made from a podium, and the difference between a proposal and a procurement is the entire investment question. The 21st East Asia Summit followed the same day, and the Prime Minister used it to name the live security problem: ships and seafarers being targeted on international waterways, alongside cyber fraud and online scams as growing cross-border crimes.

## Why the maritime plan is the one with a budget behind it

2026 is the ASEAN-India Year of Maritime Cooperation, so a long-term maritime plan is the thematic centrepiece rather than a surprise. What makes it more than a communiqué is that the procurement channel beneath it is already open and already contracting.

On 3 November the Ministry of Defence signed a ₹53,866 crore contract for eight Next Generation Corvettes for the Indian Navy, to be built by Garden Reach Shipbuilders and Goa Shipyard, replacing the Khukri and Kora class hulls by 2030. That is the single largest shipbuilding order of the year and it is the precedent that turns a maritime cooperation framework into an order book — but the order came from the Navy's own capital budget, not from Manila.

The distinction matters twice over. First, the summit did not add a rupee to the defence capital line. Second, Goa Shipyard is BSE-only and not in the NSE master file, so it cannot carry a ticker on this page. GRSE and Mazagon Dock are the listed ways to own Indian naval surface construction; Cochin Shipyard carries the refit and carrier work rather than corvettes.

## The critical-minerals mechanism is the most forward-looking item

A joint mechanism on mining and critical minerals "to build strong supply chains" is the one proposal with a genuine two-to-five-year horizon, because India's constraint on rare earths, cobalt and nickel is processing capacity rather than ore.

It is also the item most likely to be misread. ASEAN is not a source of the magnet-grade rare earths that matter — Indonesia is a nickel competitor, not a supplier to India's needs — so the mechanism is about investment, offtake and technical partnership rather than an alternative to Chinese supply. India's own National Critical Mineral Mission sits on the same problem from the domestic side, and the listed exposure is upstream: Hindustan Copper, NALCO and Mishra Dhatu, all with the caveat that none of them produces separated rare earths at scale.

The nuclear line is the same shape. ASEAN-India nuclear energy technical exchanges are the pre-commercial step before any equipment order, and India's own fleet is PHWR-based with an SMR programme still at the design stage. Heavy forging, turbines and balance-of-plant are where listed capacity exists.

## The trade number that makes this more than diplomacy

Two-way India-ASEAN trade runs at about $128.37 billion. In the six months to September, India's exports to ASEAN rose 58% year on year — the fastest-growing destination bloc in the country's trade book — and India is pressing for early conclusion of the review of the ASEAN-India Trade in Goods Agreement. 2027 will mark 35 years of dialogue relations and five years of the Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, which is why both sides are front-loading deliverables now.

Exports to ASEAN growing 58% while total merchandise exports grew 21.96% through October is the number that should carry the weight here. It says the Act East policy has already converted into shipments, which is a stronger claim than anything in Tuesday's proposal list.

## The Quad meeting is not a bilateral

The four leaders — Modi, Trump, Japan's Sanae Takaichi and Australia's Anthony Albanese — met on the sidelines in what is the first in-person Quad leaders' gathering since September 2024 and the first since Trump returned to the White House. DW reported that Modi was not expected to hold a bilateral meeting with Trump on this trip.

That is the tell on what this summit can and cannot deliver. There is no tariff announcement, no market-access deal and no India-US bilateral to price. The Indian read is defence, maritime domain awareness and Indo-Pacific alignment — all real, none of it a number. The Prime Minister did hold separate meetings with Philippines President Ferdinand Marcos Jr, Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul and Singapore's Lawrence Wong.

## What to watch

- **The AITIGA review** — early conclusion is the ask, and it is the item that would actually change tariff lines for Indian exporters. A framework without a signed goods agreement leaves the 58% growth exposed to whatever the existing rates allow.
- **Any notification of the e-visa facility** — this is the only proposal with an implementable decision inside it. A gazette or MEA notification converts it from a speech to a demand line.
- **The defence capital calendar** — Q3 contract signings through December, and the AMCA prototype down-select HAL has guided to roughly a month out.
- **26 November** — outside this file's subject but on the same calendar: the Tata Sons board meeting, with the RBI listing directive on the agenda.
- **30 November and 28 November** — Q2 FY27 GDP and October IIP, the two prints that decide whether the export momentum in this article is real or a base effect.
