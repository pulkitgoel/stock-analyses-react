The Cabinet approved two schemes today that only make sense read together. One buys the processing. The other buys the demand. Neither is large enough for what it is aiming at, and the size gap between them is the story.

**PIB does not name a single company in either release.** Every company mapping below is my own inference from historical vendor relationships and listed-sector exposure, not something the government stated. The inline markers say how strong each inference is.

## First: the smaller scheme is the strategic one

The Cabinet approved a **Capital Expenditure Support Scheme to promote critical mineral processing with an outlay of ₹2,814 crore**, running **FY 2026-27 to FY 2030-31**.

The design, from PIB and DD India's summary of the release:

- **30% capital subsidy** on eligible investment.
- **Four plants**: two lithium processing units at a minimum **30 kilotonnes** each, and two nickel processing units at a minimum **50 kilotonnes** each.
- **Caps**: ₹500 crore maximum support per lithium beneficiary, ₹900 crore per nickel beneficiary.
- **Cobalt recovered as a by-product** of nickel processing.
- **Two-stage selection** — technical qualification, then financial bidding — with two beneficiaries per mineral awarded on **least-cost** criterion.
- **Disbursed in three tranches** tied to milestones.

The government's own justification is the interesting part, because it is an admission: India *"currently relies heavily on imports of refined intermediates and high-purity materials due to limited domestic beneficiation and processing infrastructure."* It names the binding constraints explicitly — high capital requirements, limited access to technology, and raw material availability — and says the concentration of processing in a few countries *"exposes global supply chains to geopolitical disruptions, price volatility and potential supply restrictions, with implications for national security, economic sovereignty and decarbonisation targets."*

That is a supply-security argument, not an industrial-policy argument, and it is the first time this government has put money behind the *processing* step rather than the mining lease or the finished cell.

The Prime Minister's framing was **"India must have capabilities to build technologies of the future."** Worth separating the two claims there. The capability being funded is processing four plants' worth of imported ore. The technologies of the future are being bought, not built, at this stage.

## Second: the bigger scheme is eight times larger and sits downstream

The Cabinet also approved a **Viability Gap Funding scheme for grid-scale energy storage systems** — **₹14,500 crore**, **FY 2026-27 to FY 2030-31**.

The target: **92 GWh of new energy storage capacity**, which the Power Ministry describes as equivalent to **170% of India's present-day grid-scale storage capacity**. The stated requirement is **411 GWh by FY32**.

Put the two numbers next to each other. **₹14,500 crore for the demand side. ₹2,814 crore for the supply side.** The ratio is roughly **5:1** in favour of the side that consumes lithium and nickel rather than the side that produces the refined compounds. That is not a criticism of either scheme — the storage VGF is what makes the processing plants financeable, because a domestic offtake market is the precondition for a domestic processor. But it does mean India will import the cathode material for most of those 92 GWh for years yet, and the processing scheme's four plants are a start, not a solution.

There is a second constraint worth naming: the storage VGF is **viability gap funding**, which means it pays the difference between what storage costs and what the market will bear. It does not pay for the storage. If the gap is wider than ₹14,500 crore over five years, capacity lands below 92 GWh, and the scheme has a built-in incentive to award to the lowest bid rather than the most useful project.

And a third: **411 GWh by FY32 against 92 GWh funded today** leaves roughly 319 GWh to come from somewhere else. This scheme is about a fifth of the requirement.

## What would change the read

**The bid documents.** Four plants awarded on least-cost criterion, with two beneficiaries per mineral, is a small field. If a single large metals group wins both nickel slots, the scheme consolidates rather than seeds. If four separate winners emerge, the ecosystem argument holds.

**Whether the 30 kt and 50 kt figures are ore feed or refined output.** The release does not say, and the answer changes the arithmetic on whether four plants can actually serve 92 GWh. On any plausible conversion, four plants is a fraction of what 92 GWh needs.

**The December VGF guidelines.** A viability gap scheme is only as good as its gap definition. If the gap is capped low, the award goes to the bidder willing to absorb the most risk, and capacity lands under target.
