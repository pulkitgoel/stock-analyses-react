Seoul halted program selling for five minutes on Monday morning. That is the eleventh trading day this month in which an Asian government has intervened to stop a slide it did not cause.

The KOSPI fell as much as 5.8% intraday, briefly dropping below 4,600, before the Korea Exchange activated a sell-side market curb at 11:33 a.m. and suspended program selling for five minutes. Roughly ₩194 trillion — about $133 billion — of market value went with it. The index is now 48.11% below its 19 June peak, having already fallen 5.21% on Friday. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix led the decline on memory pricing.

This is the same de-rating that took the Nasdaq down 5.28% on Friday, arriving in a market with a single dominant sector and no domestic AI demand to cushion it. Korea is not falling because of Korean news. It is falling because the marginal buyer of memory has repriced the cost of capital.

The concentration explains the severity. An index whose largest weights are a foundry and two memory makers cannot absorb a repricing of AI capex the way a diversified market can.

## The policy layer, which is the only non-mechanical route to Indian IT

Here is the distinction that a chart of semiconductor prices cannot make for you.

A compression in semiconductor multiples is a discount-rate event. It does not touch the revenue line of an Indian IT services company, which is paid per engineer-hour and per managed service, and which buys almost no compute. That is why Nifty IT rose 1.69% on Monday, the only major Indian sector in the green, while the TAIEX lost 6.23%: Tata Consultancy Services +3.53%, Coforge +2.66%, Tech Mahindra +1.79%, HCLTech +1.48%, Infosys +0.49%. This "reverse AI trade" is now in its second week and it has a mechanical logic — capital rotating out of capex exposure into services that bill for labour.

What would break that rotation is regulatory, not cyclical. Washington has tightened scrutiny of data-centre operators in Singapore and four other countries in an effort to curb illegal AI chip exports to China, and is separately weighing forcing US firms to disclose their use of Chinese AI models. Both are administrative actions, which means they can land in weeks rather than through legislation. A licensing regime that constrains where American compute may be deployed is the one policy that would export the data-centre problem into the services business model.

There is a second, sharper instrument in play. Politico reported that the Office of the US Trade Representative is weighing **Section 301 investigations into the digital-trade policies of Korea, the EU and Australia**, on the argument that their rules discriminate against US technology firms. Such probes can result in tariffs. Both Korea and the EU are already inside other Section 301 tracks — Korea on forced labour (where a 12.5% duty is live) and overcapacity, the EU inside the 15-country excess-capacity probe — and Trump threatened the EU with a Section 301 inquiry earlier this year after Google was hit with two DMA fines.

The EU's position is that any tariff above the 15% cap agreed in the Turnberry deal last year breaches that agreement. Bernd Lange, who chairs the European Parliament's international trade committee, repeated on Sunday that he expects the cap to hold and that "it is absolutely clear that additional tariffs would breach the deal."

For an investor, the read is that the US tariff architecture is no longer about goods. Digital services taxes, data localisation and platform regulation are now tariffable conduct. That cuts both ways: a successful probe would be a relief for the US platforms that pay these levies, and a new 10-15% duty band for exporters in Korea and the EU.

## Anthropic: the listing held, the sentiment did not

Anthropic's first week as a public company is the cleanest available test of whether this is a pause or a repricing, and the answer so far is "both."

The issue priced at $83, traded first at $111.50, closed its first day at $97.17 for a 17% gain, then fell 8.50% to $88.91 on Friday. On Monday it traded at $86.97 at 10:30 a.m. ET, down 2.18%, still above the offer price but 22% below its first trade. On Friday it had been given the ultimate vote of confidence in the structure of a hot IPO and the opposite in the price.

The adjacent complex moved the other way. Arm extended chief executive Rene Haas's contract to 2033 and made him vice-chairman of SoftBank from 1 December. CrowdStrike agreed an all-stock merger with Zscaler that values CrowdStrike at about $43.9 billion, exchanging 1.135 CrowdStrike shares per Zscaler share — $260.41 at Friday's close, a 34% premium — with combined annual recurring revenue above $10 billion and $500 million of annualised cost synergies targeted within 18-24 months. Both boards approved. Zscaler traded up 15.61% at $225.23; CrowdStrike fell 10.32% at $205.77, which is what acquirers do.
