The most important number on Friday was not a tariff. It was **165,000**.

That is how many jobs the US economy added in October, against a consensus of roughly 59,000-65,000, and it lands four days after an election that was decided on affordability. The two facts sit badly together, and the market resolved the contradiction the only way it could: by buying the growth and paying for it in the bond market.

## First: the labour market removed the Fed's excuse

The detail, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics release:

- **Nonfarm payrolls +165,000**, against **+59,000 to +65,000 expected** depending on the survey.
- **Unemployment fell to 4.1%** from 4.2%, beating the 4.2% consensus.
- **August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000**, with September now **+60,000**.
- **Average hourly earnings +0.4% m/m, +3.1% y/y** — wage growth re-accelerating, not fading.
- **Manufacturing added 19,000 jobs.** The bulk of the gains came in professional and business services, healthcare, and transportation and warehousing.

Context that makes the print more than a beat: the **12-month average monthly gain was 48,000**, and roughly **30,000** is the level needed to hold employment steady. October was more than five times the recent trend and three times the breakeven.

The market's response was immediate and narrow. **December hike odds moved to roughly 73-80%** depending on the feed — CME FedWatch at **73%** per Barron's, Reuters at **78% from 70%** before the print. The **10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.87-4.88% (+3-4bp)**, the **30-year to 5.273%**, the **2-year to 4.49%**. The **dollar index rose 0.64% to 102.20**. Gold and bitcoin both fell.

This is a Fed already at **4.00%** with **September CPI at 3.70% y/y**, being told that the labour market is not cooperating with the disinflation story. A hike in December is now the base case rather than a tail risk.

## Second: the election changed who can investigate, not who can tax

Four days after the Democratic sweep, the shape of the tariff fight is now visible, and it is not legislative. Bloomberg's read of the day is the right one:

> Hakeem Jeffries promises to "stop the stupid Trump tariffs jacking up prices on Americans." Ron Wyden, incoming Senate Finance Committee chairman, says he'll reassert congressional authority over trade policy. Even Ted Cruz lamented that "tariffs have made everything too expensive."

And then the sentence that matters: *"curbing President Donald Trump's tariff powers won't be easy or quick."* The arithmetic has not moved since Wednesday — a veto override needs 67 senators and 290 House members, and the Democrats have neither. What they have is subpoena power, committee gavels, and the ability to make exemption-granting expensive in reputational terms.

The live channel is therefore still the executive branch. Bloomberg reports that Trump **could announce a new set of duties tied to excess industrial capacity**, covering imports from **more than a dozen economies** — the Section 301 probe launched in March against **16 economies across at least 21 manufacturing sectors**, India included. That is a duty that requires no vote, no court, and no Congress.

**The nearest hard date is Monday.** The **178 remaining Section 301 China product exclusions expire at 11:59 p.m. ET on 9 November 2026** — 164 product-specific exclusions under HTSUS **9903.88.69** and 14 solar-manufacturing-equipment exclusions under **9903.88.70**, extended by USTR's 1 December 2025 notice following the Trump-Xi agreement. **No extension has been announced.** Importers who have been planning around those lines have one weekend.

A hot jobs print that lifts chips and sinks retailers is a market saying the problem is not growth. It is the discount rate and the consumer's wallet.

## The line to take away

The election was supposed to be the tariff story. It is not, and it will not be for two more years. What changed this week is narrower and more useful: the **Fed's path got harder**, because 165,000 jobs is not an economy that needs relief; and the **tariff calendar got nearer**, because Monday's exclusion expiry was already legislated before anyone voted.

Both point the same way for equities — a higher discount rate and a higher cost base — and neither is a political event.
