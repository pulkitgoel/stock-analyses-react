## Beijing Answers the DeepSeek Ban Within a Day

The Department of Homeland Security notice published on Monday did not just restrict a company. It set a template: covered products, plus **any third-party service built using DeepSeek models**, must come out of US civilian executive-branch IT systems, and cannot be bought again.

China's commerce ministry replied on Tuesday that the order **"disregards important consensus"** reached between Xi Jinping and Trump, **"seriously hurts Chinese firms' interests"** and interferes with normal commercial cooperation, and demanded Washington **"immediately correct wrong practices... and revoke relevant discriminatory restrictions."** The ministry said Beijing is "strongly dissatisfied and firmly opposes" the move.

The timeline is the tell. The two governments agreed in September to set up a **communication channel for AI incidents**, and leaders are expected to meet at a summit in **Shenzhen next month**. The **US-China tariff truce expires January 10, 2027**, and the **30-for-30 product lists** are the first implementation test. A procurement ban on a flagship Chinese AI model, ten days after a presidential-level understanding on AI, is a negotiating position — not a rupture — but it narrows the room for the summit to produce anything concrete on technology.

For equities the transmission is asymmetric and mostly sentiment-driven. A removal mandate does not add a dollar of AI capex; it reallocates federal spending toward vendors already cleared for government work, and it hardens the argument that Chinese open-weight models carry a policy discount outside China.

## Europe Waters Down the 2035 Car Target

Three mainstream groups in the European Parliament — the centre-right **EPP**, the centre-left **S&D** and the centrist **Renew** — struck a deal early on Tuesday to weaken the EU's 2035 car emissions target.

The numbers:

- The Commission had proposed a **90% cut in CO2 from 2021 levels by 2035**, down from an earlier 100%, with the residual 10% offset by low-carbon steel and alternative fuels.
- The parliament groups instead agreed **an 87.5% cut for cars, with a 7.5% top-up from production using low-carbon steel made in the EU**.
- **Vans: an 80% cut by 2035.**
- **2030 targets: 55% for cars (unchanged) and 35% for vans, down from the Commission's 40%.**
- Automakers may meet the **2030** targets on a **2029-2031 average**.
- Cars running on **renewable fuels** can be recognised as zero-emission vehicles, and **small affordable EVs and particularly efficient EVs earn super-credits**.

France and Germany had already agreed on October 16 to a 90% target with no compensatory measures, so the parliament text is weaker than the two largest car-producing member states wanted. It still needs the three groups to sign off formally, and then agreement with member states in Council.

The investment read is straightforward: this is a delay and a dilution of the powertrain transition, which protects near-term margin at legacy manufacturers and pushes out the point at which battery-electric capacity must be paid for.

## Asia Sold Off, and Two Data Points Explain Why

Tuesday's Asian session gave back part of Wall Street's Monday rebound:

- **Nikkei 225 -1.3% to 64,075.42**
- **Hang Seng -1.1% to 23,927.47**
- **Shanghai Composite -0.1% to 3,726.38**

The macro backdrop was already mixed. China's **third-quarter GDP grew 4.5% year on year** — above the 4.4% forecast and the second quarter's 4.3%, and **1.2% quarter on quarter** — but the composition was exports up, consumers reluctant, which is the least reassuring version of a 4.5% print for anyone selling into Chinese demand.

Two company-level confirmations landed on Tuesday:

- **CATL's third-quarter profit growth was its slowest in nearly two years** as competition in batteries intensifies — the single best proxy for the EV supply chain's pricing power.
- **Elice Group, a South Korean AI data-centre firm, fell nearly 30% on its trading debut** after raising 201.1 billion won (USD 151 million), closing below its IPO price. That is the cleanest available test of retail appetite for AI-infrastructure paper, and it failed.

Add the regional flashpoints — China's navy shadowing a US warship near **Sabina Shoal**, and the **UK deploying jets to defend Saudi Arabia from Houthi attacks** — and the oil market's refusal to break below $100 starts to look structural rather than headline-driven. Brent touched a near **five-month high** during Tuesday's session before reversing to trade about **1.3% lower** around **$108**, with WTI near **$103**.

## Aviation: A MAX Certification and a Warning About Fares

Two aviation items on the same day, pointing in opposite directions:

- The **US is set to announce certification of the Boeing 737 MAX 10 on Tuesday**, according to sources — the last of the MAX variants to clear the process, and the one that unlocks the densest, highest-margin seat configuration for narrowbody operators.
- **Ryanair's Michael O'Leary told Europe to prepare for the biggest airfare increase in history**, with fuel above $100 feeding directly into ticket prices. That is a demand-elasticity warning dressed as a pricing opportunity.

The follow-through for suppliers is the one to watch: **GE Aerospace** explicitly flagged on Tuesday that prolonged high fuel prices could push airlines to cut flying, which would weaken aftermarket demand for engine repairs and spares — the most profitable line in aerospace.

## Read-Across for India

India has no direct exposure to either European car rules or the DeepSeek procurement ban, but two channels matter:

- **Indian IT services** sit on the wrong side of an AI-decoupling escalation in the short term: less cross-border data work, more scrutiny of delivery models that touch Chinese-origin tooling. The offset is that every US federal removal-and-replace programme is integration work.
- **Indian refiners** are the marginal buyer of every discounted barrel the West will not take, so a Brent marker stuck above $100 and a Venezuela-shaped supply solution are both live variables for them this week.
