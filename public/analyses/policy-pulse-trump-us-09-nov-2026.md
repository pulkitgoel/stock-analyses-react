Two numbers crossed in Washington this week and only one of them is a policy.

This weekend the President warned reporters that foreign companies which do not build factories in the United States could face tariffs of up to 300%, and that "many measures" would follow over the next week or two. He named no legal basis, no country list and no product list, and no order setting a 300% rate has been issued. Aju Press, SBS and NOCUT all carry the same caveat, and Seoul Economic Daily's read is blunt: the figure reflects rhetoric rather than a measure.

On Friday, the Office of the US Trade Representative did the dull thing that actually moves a landed cost. It extended all 178 Section 301 China product exclusions — 164 ordinary product lines under HTSUS 9903.88.69 and 14 solar-manufacturing-equipment lines under 9903.88.70 — from tonight's 11:59 p.m. ET expiry to 11:59 p.m. ET on 9 January 2027, covering goods entered from 12:01 a.m. on 10 November. A separate maritime notice suspends the Section 301 port fees over the same window.

So the headline rate went up and the operative rate did not. An importer's duty line was set on Friday and is unchanged by today's press availability.

## What sixty days buys

The extension is short. A multi-association letter to Ambassador Greer on 4 November asked for at least a year, arguing that for many of these inputs "sourcing options outside China are limited or non-existent". They got two months.

That is the calendar, not careless drafting. The US-China arrangement was extended in late September from 10 November to 10 January 2027, and the exclusions now run to 9 January — one day inside the truce, not past it. Every extension since 2022 has been a rolling short grant announced days before the previous expiry; this is the shortest of the series.

The notice also contradicts itself on the end date. Its Dates section and both annexes say 9 January, while other text in the same notice says 10 January. Work to the 9th: entries planned around the 10th pay full duty.

Tomorrow is the other half of the bargain. China's suspension of its October 2025 extraterritorial rare-earth export controls expires on 10 November. If Beijing lets it lapse, the magnet complex re-prices within a session and the 9 January date stops being the binding constraint on anybody's supply chain.

## The channel that is actually tightening

The tariff argument is loud and slow. Export enforcement is quiet, fast, and it is where the semiconductor risk now sits.

- On 4 November the Financial Times reported that Oracle cancelled a $7 billion, five-year AI-chip lease with Tencent — up to 100,000 advanced accelerators drawn from South-East Asian data centres — after US officials raised concerns. That deal existed because renting Nvidia compute offshore is legal where buying the chip is not.
- Nvidia has told cloud customers that at least 51% of capacity on new contracts must go to non-Chinese buyers, per the same reporting.
- On Sunday the Nvidia board opened an independent investigation, overseen by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel, after reports that founder and CEO Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The reports concern Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman and former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a company US authorities have examined since 2024 over restricted hardware reaching China. The board says it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised"; Huang's memo to employees acknowledges he was unfaithful to his wife and states that he supports the review. No new regulation has been announced.

That sequence matters for how the risk gets priced. A rule can be modelled, dated and hedged. Pressure applied to a counterparty cannot. Reuters' read, carried by the Korea Herald, is that Samsung and SK hynix face a higher export-compliance burden without any new rule existing — the cost arrives as diligence, not as a duty line.

## The veto wall

The midterm result changed the noise, not the arithmetic. Democrats take both chambers — Reuters' projection puts the House at 235 seats or more — and plan a "first-100-hours" vote to reclaim congressional authority over tariffs, starting with the 50% Section 338 duties on Canadian goods. They will pass the House. They will not reach 67 in the Senate or 290 in the House, and the President has said he would veto. Section 232 investigations into robotics, industrial machinery, wind turbines and medical goods remain open in the meantime.

What Democrats can actually do is oversight, and the useful part is narrow. Representative Jamie Raskin's 28 October request for documents on tariff exemptions goes to whether relief was granted to companies that had given the President gifts. No wrongdoing has been established. But an exemption investigation is a live policy risk for any company holding one — which is the one place where the election result reaches a stock price this quarter.
