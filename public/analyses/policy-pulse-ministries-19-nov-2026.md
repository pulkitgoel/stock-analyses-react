On the same day the government handed letters of intent to twenty-five companies to manufacture telecom equipment in Gwalior, the defence ministry signed a cheque for ₹1,976.40 crore to an Israeli firm to upgrade a missile system already installed on Indian warships.

Both are Make in India. Only one of them involved an Indian supplier.

## Twenty-five letters, no names

The Department of Telecommunications, with the Madhya Pradesh government, distributed letters of intent to 25 companies for the Telecom Manufacturing Zone at Gwalior at an event in Bhopal. Phase-II of the zone was announced at the same ceremony. The zone runs to roughly 170 acres — land the state cabinet approved — and is intended for telecom equipment, components, optical fibre, software and passive infrastructure.

The numbers behind it: earlier investor outreach in Delhi and Mumbai produced ₹5,500 crore of commitments with a potential 20,000 jobs, and the zone is now converting interest into allotments. Union Communications Minister Jyotiraditya Scindia framed it as a shift "from being a services nation to becoming a product nation."

PIB does not name a single one of the 25 companies. That is not a small omission for anyone trying to trade it. The read-through has to be built from the categories the zone covers, and every name below is inference.

## The missile that was bought, not built

The defence ministry's contract with Israel Aerospace Industries covers upgradation and obsolescence management for the Barak point defence missile system on Indian Navy ships, at an overall cost of ₹1,976.40 crore. It was signed on 18 November under the Other Capital Procurement Procedure in the presence of Defence Secretary Rajesh Kumar Singh, and covers a comprehensive hardware and software upgrade plus obsolescence management, for "enhanced features and potent range anti-air defence capability."

This is the procurement route that exists precisely because there is no indigenous alternative certified for the role. Barak is an Israeli system; the upgrade work will not be done by an Indian prime. The ₹1,976 crore is small against a ₹2.19 lakh crore capital line, but it is the clearest illustration of the problem the defence secretary described at FICCI on Wednesday: the constraint is not the budget, it is what the budget can be spent on domestically.

On the same day the defence minister was in Tokyo for the fourth India-Japan 2+2 foreign and defence ministerial meeting. That is a co-production conversation, and it will take years to produce a contract.

## ₹8,981 crore of Coal India leaving the government

The Mahanadi Coalfields offer for sale opened for subscription and is the largest primary-market event of the week. Coal India is selling 66.18 crore shares, a 10% stake, at ₹129-136, for ₹8,981.12 crore at the top of the band. It closes on 23 November, allotment is 25 November, and listing is expected on 27 November.

The anchor book took ₹2,295.2 crore — 16.87 crore shares to 53 institutional investors at ₹136. LIC took more than 26% of the anchor allocation and Nippon Life India close to ₹500 crore.

The valuation argument is a dividend one. At the IPO price the trailing yield is 8.5%, against 6.6% for Coal India itself in FY26, and MCL is being marketed at a 10-15% premium to Coal India's roughly 5 times FY26 multiple — a premium PL Capital justifies on MCL's 41.7% EBITDA margin, 53.8% return on net worth and 6% EBITDA CAGR against Coal India's 5%.

Two things follow. First, none of the ₹8,981 crore goes to Mahanadi Coalfields; it all goes to Coal India, which is a cash event for the parent and a supply event for the stock. Second, the grey market premium was ₹3.10 a share on Thursday morning, about 2.3% over the top of the band — a listing gain roughly the size of a rounding error, which is itself the signal about how the issue is being priced.

## Two smaller items with real tickers

NLC India Renewables filed its draft red herring prospectus for an IPO with a ₹3,300 crore fresh issue alongside an offer for sale, to fund solar, wind, storage and future green energy projects. That is a renewables carve-out from a listed PSU, and it puts a separate valuation on the growth assets without the coal drag.

Adani Energy Solutions won a Bihar transmission project tied to a 2,400 MW power plant, taking its transmission capital expenditure plan to ₹91,500 crore. Transmission is the part of the Adani complex with contracted, regulated returns rather than merchant exposure, which is why the announcement is being read as a capex visibility item rather than a growth headline.

The road transport ministry also revised the validity periods for pollution-under-control certificates by vehicle age and emission standard, allowing up to three years for BS-VI passenger vehicles. It reduces a small recurring compliance friction and is not a margin event for anyone.

## What to watch

- **23 November** — the Mahanadi Coalfields issue closes. Watch the retail and NII categories rather than the QIB book, which is already anchored at ₹2,295 crore.
- **25 and 27 November** — allotment and listing. A listing at or below the band would be the first soft PSU disinvestment print of the fiscal year.
- **26 November** — the Tata Sons board meets on the RBI's NBFC listing directive.
- **1 December** — the SEBI commodity margin framework takes effect, cutting agricultural initial margin to 6%, 8% and 10%.
- **4 December** — Section 232 polysilicon measures bite: 15% ad valorem plus minimum import prices of $21/kg for polysilicon, $100/kg for ingots and wafers, $0.22/W for cells and $0.38/W for modules. That stacks on the India anti-dumping findings at roughly 249% combined and is the next dated instrument for the solar manufacturing chain.
- **Around 1 December** — the AMCA winner, on the defence secretary's one-month guidance from 18 November.
