There are two tariff stories in the United States today and only one of them is about trade. The other is about Maine and Michigan.

## First: the Canada tariff wall is being rebuilt, not dismantled

**President Trump signed a proclamation on Friday, October 23, further modifying the scope of the 50% Section 338 duties on Canadian goods, effective October 30, 2026.** Treatment turns on the entry date, so the timing of entry for consumption or withdrawal from warehouse decides which side of the line a shipment falls on.

What comes out of scope, and what goes in:

| Removed from the 50% scope | Added to the 50% scope |
|---|---|
| Plywood sheets | Certain valves |
| Some paper products | Carpets |
| Certain machinery and refrigeration equipment | Waste products, including sewage sludge and some municipal waste |
| Certain packing crates and boxes | Cocoa powder |
| | Paints and varnishes |
| | Shampoos |
| | Vinyl flooring |
| | Archery equipment |

**Read the list twice and the politics is obvious.** Senator Susan Collins, in a tight re-election race in Maine, said Friday that nine categories of Canadian exports would be exempted — corrugated paper, plastic boxes and several types of machinery her state's blueberry, potato, apple and seaweed producers depend on. Her framing was explicitly about inputs "either unavailable from American suppliers or cannot be replaced without incurring significant expense." Ontario Premier Doug Ford's response on social media was four words: "Now get rid of the rest."

**The same order that grants that relief imposes a 50% duty on sewage sludge and some municipal waste, and newly tariffs cocoa powder, paints, varnishes, shampoos, vinyl flooring, carpets and archery equipment.** This is not a de-escalation. It is a reallocation of pain toward products with less electoral weight in two Senate races that decide control of the chamber — Democrats need a net gain of four seats.

Trump, at a rally on the Michigan border on Sunday, said a Canada trade deal is coming "soon." The proclamation he signed two days earlier added fourteen product categories to a duty wall. Both things are true at once, and the market should believe the signed document over the rally line.

The legal flank is closing in parallel. A US food importer filed a challenge this week against Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — the Depression-era statute Trump revived for these duties, the first use of it in roughly a century. The standing exemptions remain: energy products, potash, goods already under Section 232, civil aircraft, fish and critical minerals.

## Second: the disclosure that matters is the oil and gas one

**The Joint Economic Committee minority released an analysis today finding Trump's personal wealth from oil and gas stocks has grown by as much as $35.8 million so far this year, taking his net holdings in the sector to as much as $81.6 million — nearly double what he held at the end of 2025.**

Two numbers in that report are the ones to keep:

- **94% of Trump's 2026 oil and gas stock purchases occurred after the Iran war began**, with as much as **$17.5 million** bought after February 28.
- **Americans have spent an additional $101 billion on gasoline** since the war started, an average of **$856 in added cost per family.**

This lands the same weekend Trump told a Georgia rally he does not "give a damn" that people are suffering at the pump. The prior June filing already showed his accounts selling between $500,000 and $1 million of ExxonMobil.

**Treat this as a policy-durability signal, not a copy trade.** The actionable inference is not "buy what he bought" — it is that the occupant of the White House has a disclosed financial interest in crude staying where it is, at the same time he is the person who decides whether the Strait of Hormuz risk premium decays. That is a reason to discount de-escalation headlines, and a reason to keep fuel-cost exposure light.

## Third: DJT is at a record low and the API story cannot rescue it

**Trump Media & Technology Group fell about 4% in early trading to roughly $6.16, extending a slide that took it to a 52-week low of $6.40 on October 19.** The stock is down more than 50% year on year, against a 52-week high of $17.09 in December 2025. It closed Friday at $6.43, down 8%.

The company had one genuine revenue catalyst this year — the Truth API paid institutional data feed at $100,000 a month, launched August 1 — and it is now carrying a legal overhang on top: The Intercept and the Freedom of the Press Foundation sued in Manhattan federal court over the early-access feed. Truth Social's daily active users were around 260,000 in September, down roughly 30% year on year.
