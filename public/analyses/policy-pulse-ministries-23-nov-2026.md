Foreign investors pulled ₹5.61 lakh crore — about $62.24 billion — out of Indian equities between October 2024 and October 2026. On Friday evening SEBI published a two-page question asking whether the disclosure rule that helped push them out should be scrapped.

The consultation paper, dated 20 November, proposes removing the Additional Disclosure Framework for foreign portfolio investors entirely. It extends to subscribers of Offshore Derivative Instruments and to segregated portfolios maintained by an FPI. Public comments close on 11 December 2026, and the paper asks one question: should the ADF be removed?

## What the framework actually required

The ADF sits on top of the beneficial-ownership rules, not in place of them. It bites when an FPI meets either of two tests: it holds more than 50% of its Indian equity assets under management in a single Indian corporate group, or its equity AUM individually or with its investor group exceeds ₹50,000 crore. The size threshold was raised from ₹25,000 crore in April 2025.

An FPI inside those triggers must disclose every entity and natural person holding any ownership, economic interest or control in it, on a full look-through basis, with no minimum threshold at all. Certain broad-based pooled vehicles with wide investor bases, and government-related investors, are exempt subject to conditions.

SEBI's argument is comparative and specific. Under India's Prevention of Money Laundering Rules the beneficial-ownership threshold is 10% or 15% depending on the legal form of the client. The United States, the UK, Canada, Germany, Singapore, Japan and Hong Kong generally use 25%. The ADF goes further than any of them, and SEBI says the requirement is "unique to India" — global funds must collect data on far more investors for India than for any other market, hedge funds face repeated look-through exercises as their investor base churns, and multi-layered fund-of-fund structures become prohibitive.

⚠️ **One correction to a great deal of the coverage.** Several reports have folded this into a separate proposal to raise India's beneficial-ownership threshold to 25%. They are different things. The 25% figure comes from the PMLR rules, which are the Ministry of Finance's jurisdiction, not SEBI's — the consultation paper says so explicitly and notes only that "there may be a need to review the PMLR, 2005." This paper proposes removing the extra layer above those thresholds. Nothing in it changes the 10%/15% regime.

## Why this is a flow story, not an earnings story

The regulatory change moves no company's revenue. What it can move is the direction and durability of foreign flows, and the scale of the reversal already underway is the point.

Foreign portfolio investors have bought roughly ₹1.17 lakh crore of Indian equities so far in November, on the arithmetic of ₹84,291 crore net in the 1-15 November NSDL fortnight and ₹32,646.67 crore net in the week to 20 November. That follows two months of heavy selling, and it is the single largest reason the Nifty went from a nine-month low to a nine-month high in four weeks.

The buying was not evenly spread. Financial services took more than half the fortnight's all-sector net inflow, with telecommunications, automobiles, consumer services and information technology the other major beneficiaries. Five of the 22 named industry sectors were still net sellers. That is a useful corrective to "FIIs are back": they are back in banks.

The counterweight is political. The Congress party said on Sunday that the proposals open a window to foreign funds with opaque offshore ownership structures and should be buried, arguing that "ease of doing business cannot come at the cost of capital market integrity." That is a live objection and it is aimed squarely at the transparency-for-inflows trade the paper makes.

## The rest of the day was data, and the data was good

Three releases, all pointing the same way, and the market ignored all of them.

The **HSBC Flash India Composite PMI** for November came in at 56.3 against October's 56.2 — a five-month high, the highest since June's 57.1, and the 64th consecutive month above 50. The manufacturing PMI rose to a nine-month high of 56.0 from 54.7 on stronger orders, production and hiring. Manufacturing output jumped to 58.3 from 57.0. But services slipped to 55.3 from 55.8, and the employment split is the interesting part: factories hired at the fastest pace in seven months while services firms cut headcount for the first time in nearly a year. Input-cost inflation stayed subdued; output prices rose modestly, which supports margins.

The **Index of Core Industries** for October grew 10.9% year on year against a revised 8.2% in September — the first double-digit reading under the 2022-23 base series. The index stood at 123.1 against 111.0. Iron ore led at +26.4%, then coal at +23.1%, electricity at +21.6% (its strongest reading under the new series, from 12.6%), cement at +15.4%, steel at +7.3% and refinery products at +3.4%. Three sectors contracted: fertilisers -12.2%, its eighth straight month of decline; natural gas -7.6%, the steepest fall under the new series; and crude oil -4.1%. Cumulative April-October growth was 6.0% against 2.3% a year earlier.

Electricity carries 30.9% of the index weight, so a 21.6% electricity print is doing most of the work in the headline. Read the composite as an electricity-and-construction number, not as broad industrial strength. Fertiliser's eight-month contraction is the cleanest negative in the set.

And the **Mahanadi Coalfields offer for sale closed undersubscribed.** At 11:41 a.m. the ₹8,981.12 crore issue was at 0.58 times, with QIBs at 1.45 times, non-institutional investors at 0.29 times and retail at 0.34 times. The grey market premium had collapsed from ₹3.65 on 17 November to ₹0.45 by Monday morning. This is Coal India selling 10% of its largest subsidiary, priced at ₹129-136 a share against FY26 revenue of ₹33,899 crore and profit of ₹10,698 crore — cheap by any reading — and institutional money still would not take it in size. The "IPO boom lifts exchange volumes" thesis needs a footnote now.

## The metal index, and one deal that explains it

Nifty Metal was the day's worst sector at -3.68%, and most of that is a single transaction.

Tata Steel has agreed to sell its steel tubes business and 100% of Tata Steel Colors to APL Apollo Tubes for ₹28,000 crore — ₹11,700 crore for the tubes division (Jamshedpur, Hosur, Khopoli and Sahibabad; FY26 turnover about ₹11,375 crore) and ₹16,300 crore for Colors, formerly Tata BlueScope (FY26 revenue about ₹4,680 crore, profit ₹85 crore). In the same structure Tata Steel is buying the 26.6% promoter stake in APL Apollo from S Gupta Holding at ₹2,100 a share for ₹15,510 crore, and making a mandatory open offer for a further 26% at ₹2,301.40 a share, worth up to ₹16,756 crore. Fully accepted, that takes Tata Steel to 52.38% and makes it APL Apollo's promoter.

The market disliked both sides, for opposite reasons. APL Apollo closed at ₹1,670, down 21.3% from ₹2,121.60, a fresh 52-week low, on 2.6 crore shares — its steepest single-session fall in more than six years — because it is funding a ₹28,000 crore cash acquisition from a net cash position of about ₹1,530 crore at FY26. Tata Steel fell 8.43% to ₹163.00 as the biggest Sensex loser, because it is paying ₹15,510 crore for a stake while selling the downstream assets that generated the value. Completion is targeted by 31 March 2028 and needs regulatory approvals.
