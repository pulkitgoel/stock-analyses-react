Two ministry numbers published within a week of each other describe the same economy from opposite ends: what a household no longer pays for its own healthcare, and what a shareholder is about to be asked to buy.

The first is the National Health Accounts 2023-24, released by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare on 15 November. The second is the Mahanadi Coalfields offer for sale, which opens on 19 November and will move ₹8,981 crore out of Coal India and into public hands.

## 42.7%, and the year-over-year number nobody quotes

The headline in the NHA release is that out-of-pocket expenditure fell from 64.2% of total health spending in 2013-14 to 42.7% in 2023-24 — a 21.5 percentage point fall in a decade. Government health expenditure rose from ₹1,29,778 crore to ₹4,14,196 crore, about 3.2 times, and per capita government spending from ₹1,042 to ₹2,959.

Those are real numbers and they describe a genuine shift in who pays. Social security expenditure on health went from 6% to 11% of the total, and private health insurance from 3.4% to 10.2%. Government spending on primary healthcare was roughly ₹1.53 lakh crore.

Now the number the release does not lead with: out-of-pocket spending was **39.4%** in 2021-22, then **43.4%** in 2022-23, and is **42.7%** now. The decade-long decline is real, but the latest year moved by 0.7 percentage points and the two years before it moved the wrong way, because pandemic-era government spending had temporarily inflated the government share.

Two further caveats, both of which matter more than the headline:

- A share is not an amount. Out-of-pocket spending can fall as a percentage of the total while rising in rupees, because the total is growing.
- The GDP yardstick is not in this release. Government health expenditure was 1.84% of GDP in 2021-22, against the National Health Policy 2017 target of 2.5% by 2025. On the last published ratio, the target was missed.

What the release does establish is a payer migration. Medicines and outpatient care — neither covered by PM-JAY, which pays only for hospitalisation — remain the largest component of out-of-pocket spending, and the "missing middle" of informal workers too well-off for PM-JAY and too poorly covered for private insurance is still exposed.

## The other number: ₹8,981 crore of Coal India

Coal India's offer for sale of Mahanadi Coalfields opens on 19 November and closes on 23 November, with allotment on 25 November and a tentative listing on 27 November.

The structure matters more than the size. It is 66.18 crore shares at ₹129 to ₹136 — entirely an offer for sale by Coal India, with no fresh capital to the company. At the top of the band that is ₹8,981.12 crore, implying a post-issue market capitalisation of ₹90,009.74 crore, or roughly $9.55 billion, which would make it one of the larger listings of the year. The lot is 110 shares, or ₹14,960 at the top of the band.

The underlying business is unusually simple to value. MCL produced 218.31 million tonnes in FY26 — 21% of India's domestic coal and 28.4% of Coal India's own output — against audited reserves of 9,840.31 million tonnes as of 1 April 2026. FY26 total income was ₹33,899 crore with profit after tax of ₹10,698 crore. For the six months to 30 September 2026, revenue was ₹17,107.83 crore and profit after tax ₹4,880.66 crore. Borrowings were ₹2.76 crore against net worth of ₹22,399.80 crore.

At ₹136, that is about 8.4 times trailing FY26 earnings. A near-debt-free monopoly-adjacent coal producer at 8.4 times is cheap on the face of it, and the discount is the point: single-buyer exposure to thermal power, regulated fuel supply agreement pricing, and top-line growth of 3% in FY26.

Two things follow for the parent. First, ₹8,981 crore of Coal India stock is being sold into the market over five sessions — a supply event, not a demand event. Second, the issue prices MCL at 8.4 times earnings, which becomes the reference multiple anyone valuing Coal India's remaining subsidiaries will now use.

## The third item: a probe, not a duty

On 9 November, the Directorate General of Trade Remedies opened a suo motu anti-dumping investigation into aluminium extrusion profiles, structures and articles from China, Malaysia, Vietnam and Thailand — case AD/OI/079/2026 on the SETU portal.

This one is worth stating precisely because it is easy to overstate. DGTR initiated on its own initiative after representations from the Aluminium Extrusion Manufacturers Association of India, which represents 228 manufacturers, mostly MSMEs. The four countries accounted for about 90% of India's imports of the goods under investigation. The dumping period is 1 April 2025 to 30 June 2026.

**No duty has been recommended and none has been imposed.** An initiation is the start of an examination. Interested parties have 15 days from DGTR's letters for scope comments and 37 days for questionnaire responses, after which a finding — if any — goes to the Finance Ministry, which decides independently.

The stock read is therefore second-order, and it cuts both ways. Indian aluminium producers gain pricing support if a duty lands; the same companies' downstream extrusion businesses, and every listed user of extruded aluminium in construction, solar mounting and transport equipment, pay more for inputs. Most of the affected extrusion capacity on the Indian side is unlisted MSME, which is exactly why DGTR acted on its own.

## What to watch

- **19-23 November** — the Mahanadi Coalfields subscription window, with allotment on 25 November and listing on 27 November. Watch the QIB book on day one: a 100% offer for sale with a 5% grey market premium is a demand test, not a valuation test.
- **Within 15 and 37 days of DGTR's intimation letters** — scope comments and questionnaire responses in the aluminium extrusion case. A finding is a recommendation; only a Customs notification creates a duty.
- **28 November** — the next IIP release for October, and **30 November** — Q2 FY27 GDP. September IIP came in at 6.7%, a four-month low, with mining contracting 6.2%.
- **The next NHA round** — the number to watch is not the decade-long fall but whether out-of-pocket share resumes declining after two years of moving the other way.
