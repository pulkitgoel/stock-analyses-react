The tariff is still a leak, not an instrument, and that distinction is doing all the work today.

The Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday morning that the administration is preparing to raise duties on China, the European Union and 14 other economies, on the argument that government subsidies have let their manufacturers overproduce and then undercut American producers. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer could publish the findings as soon as this week. USTR declined to comment. Its press-release index still carries nothing newer than 19 November, so at the time of writing there is no determination, no rate, no product list and no effective date.

What the reporting does give us is the architecture, and the architecture is the tradeable part. The investigation was opened on 11 March 2026 under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, against 16 economies: China, the EU, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan and India. Together they accounted for close to $2.4 trillion of US goods imports last year — just over 70% of total US merchandise imports, per the Tax Foundation's Erica York. The sectors named in the probe are steel, aluminium, vehicles, batteries, chemicals, electronics, machinery, semiconductors, solar modules, ships and transport equipment.

Three details decide how much of that becomes duty.

First, carve-outs. Steel, aluminium and automobiles are expected to be excluded because they already carry separate national-security tariffs under Section 232. That removes the largest and most obvious US-listed beneficiaries of a tariff wall from the trade.

Second, tiering. Countries with negotiated agreements — Japan, Korea, the EU — are expected to land at 15% or below, because the administration has said it will honour those deals. India and Vietnam are expected to be higher, on the reported logic that their agreements leave more room. China is expected to be highest. The new duties would sit on top of the 10%–12.5% forced-labour tariffs that took effect on 24 July, which are themselves under challenge at the Court of International Trade.

Third, an escalator. Officials are reportedly drafting a provision that would allow rates to rise further if a country's overcapacity worsens or if it fails to change policy in future negotiations. A tariff with an automatic ratchet is a different instrument from a tariff with a review.

So the sequencing is: a tariff cost that a large importer can quantify, absorb and still beat on, is a margin problem the market will look through. A tariff cost that has no rate attached yet is not priced at all. Neither is a reason to buy the sector, and neither is a reason to sell it today.

## Crypto lost the bid it had borrowed

Bitcoin traded near $59,750 after touching a 26-month low over the weekend, having slipped back below $60,000 on Tuesday morning. It is down roughly 25% on the month and has now given back the entire gain that followed the November 2024 election — the move that was, at the time, the single cleanest expression of the "crypto president" trade.

The flows explain more than the price. Spot bitcoin ETFs recorded about $570 million of outflows on Monday, after four consecutive weeks that removed more than $4 billion, and November outflows have reached a record $4.71 billion. Higher real rates do the rest: the market is pricing a December hike, and the 10-year at 4.91% is not a friendly discount rate for a non-yielding asset.

The structural story is Strategy. Michael Saylor's company disclosed in an 8-K that it holds 851,305 bitcoin bought for about $64.23 billion at an average of $75,446 a coin. Against a spot price near $60,000 that is a paper loss of about $12.9 billion, and at a market capitalisation near $35.7 billion the equity is now worth roughly 70 cents for every dollar of bitcoin on the balance sheet.

That inverts the machine. Strategy built the treasury by selling stock at a premium to its coins; each sale raised bitcoin per share. Below the value of the coins, each new share sold dilutes holders instead of adding exposure — and last week the company did exactly that, selling 1.93 million shares for $176.7 million of net proceeds while spending $211.8 million buying back its STRC preferred and only $29.6 million on 457 bitcoin. It also raised its preferred repurchase authorisation to $4 billion from $3 billion. MSCI is scheduled to delete the stock from its US indexes at the close on 30 November, which forces index funds to sell.

## The President's own position in the chipmaker

The disclosure that landed on 19 November is the September periodic transaction report: 25 pages, 751 transactions, between roughly $40.9 million and $148.1 million in aggregate, with purchases of at least $29.2 million and sales of at least $11.7 million. It was signed on 1 November, received by the Office of Government Ethics on 5 November and published on the 19th.

Inside it: purchases of Nvidia, Palantir and SpaceX in September, around the time all three were in the news for federal contracts and presidential meetings. Two Nvidia purchases on 2 September worth up to $350,000 combined landed the same day Nvidia signed its agreement to acquire Hugging Face for about $12.9 billion, which was announced the following day. Tesla purchases of $117,004 to $330,000. SpaceX equity in small slices plus $100,001–$250,000 of SpaceX 5.35% senior notes.

The sales are the bigger numbers. On 23 September the accounts sold $1 million to $5 million each of McDonald's, Home Depot, Comcast and Fidelity National Information Services, and $500,001 to $1 million each of Nike and Constellation Brands. The single largest transaction that day was a purchase of $5 million to $25 million of the Vanguard Dividend Appreciation ETF.

The aggregate Nvidia position is the part that matters this week. CNBC's count from the disclosure forms is 103 separate purchases or sales of Nvidia in the second term, worth $27 million to $97.5 million in total. The Washington Post put the holding at $13 million to $62 million at the end of August; the annual filing for end-2025 had it at $12.5 million to $58 million, one of his three largest single-stock positions. The Financial Times estimated he could have held between 32,000 and 370,000 shares at the end of September — which, on Monday's 31% move, would have been a gain of $1.3 million to $15 million.

The White House position is that the portfolio is managed by outside institutions replicating indexes including the Schwab 1000, and that neither the president nor his family directs it. There is no evidence in the filing that he did. The compliance question is narrower and unanswered: the STOCK Act requires covered transactions to be reported within 30 days of learning of them and no later than 45 days after the trade, and this report covers transactions from 1 September that were signed on 1 November.

**Read this as a disclosure, not a recommendation.** I am not asserting that any purchase was informed by policy. What is documented is a position, a date and a price move; what is alleged by critics is the link. The distinction is the whole of the story.

## Iran got a calendar

Trump told Fox News that he is inclined to resume strikes on Iran after the New Year if no deal is reached, and that the US will "see what happens" after 1 January. Israeli reporting via Ynet said Washington has decided for now to delay any attack by at least a week, against Israeli pressure for a joint campaign. Iran, for its part, unveiled an underground missile facility on 21 November.

A pause with a date is not de-escalation; it is a deadline with the risk premium moved out of the current quarter. Brent at $87.46 is already well below the $100-plus prints of September, so the war premium has largely bled out. The asymmetry is that a deal removes very little further downside from crude, while a lapse in the pause — or a new Iranian test of the blockade — puts it straight back.

Today's tape is not trading that either: energy is up with the rotation (Exxon +0.98%, Chevron +1.54%, Occidental +1.21%) while the airlines are down on fuel (Delta -0.71%, United -1.53%).
