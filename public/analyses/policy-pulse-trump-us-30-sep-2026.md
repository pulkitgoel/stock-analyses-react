## Core PCE Cools to 3.0% and the October Odds Flip — From 71% to 35%

**The August personal income and outlays report, released on the morning of September 30, was the first inflation print since the Fed hiked on September 16 — and it went the dovish way.** The PCE price index rose **0.3% on the month and 3.4% on the year**, against consensus estimates of 0.4% and 3.7%. **Core PCE — the measure the Fed actually targets — rose 0.2% on the month and 3.0% on the year**, against 0.3% and 3.3% expected. Headline and core came in cooler in the same direction for the first time in months.

The repricing was immediate and sized:

- **CME FedWatch moved to a 65.1% probability that the Fed holds at the late-October meeting, against 34.9% for another 25bp hike.** A week earlier the same tool showed a **70.9% probability of a hike**; on September 29 the market was split roughly evenly. That is a swing of roughly 36 points, and most of it happened after the 8:30 a.m. ET release.
- **Treasury yields fell, led by the front end.** Short-dated yields took the move as traders re-dated the next hike toward December; the long end eased after touching multi-decade highs (see below).
- **US equities rose**, with the Nasdaq leading. The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq remained on course for a **second consecutive quarterly gain** despite September's drawdown.
- **ADP private payrolls came in at +90,000 for September**, up from a downwardly revised **36,000** in August and above the **68,000** consensus — a labour market that is neither cracking nor re-accelerating.
- **The personal savings rate fell to 4.1% from 4.6%**, with consumer spending surging. That is why the softer inflation read is not an unambiguous easing signal.

Bill Adams of Fifth Third framed the measurement problem exactly: inflation is closer to target "because of how inflation is measured, not how it's trending… You don't need your glasses on to tell the difference between 3% core PCE inflation and a 2% target." Chris Zaccarelli of Northlight drew the policy conclusion the market is trading: the data suggests **"the Fed was probably correct in raising rates this month, but if the inflation data improves, they might be able to skip a meeting or at least raise rates less than the three times in a row that many were worried about."**

**The steel angle landed two days earlier.** On **September 28** the President announced a **$15 billion Mesabi Metallics steel mill in eastern Iowa** — **7.5 million short tons** of capacity in its first year, ramping toward **10 million**, **1,750 permanent jobs**, iron ore from a Mesabi mine in Minnesota, and production targeted for **2030**. Trump called it the largest steel plant in US history and the first mega-mill built since the 1960s, and credited the **50% steel tariffs** for the investment. It is also the domestic backdrop to the **Global Forum on Steel Excess Capacity Ministerial** that opened the G20 Trade Ministerial in Milwaukee on September 30 (covered in the Global edition).

## Section 301 Returns to Court: 99.4% of US Imports Before Three Judges

**On the morning of September 30 a three-judge panel at the US Court of International Trade in Manhattan heard oral argument in *In Re: Section 301 Forced Labor Cases*** — the consolidated challenge to the tariffs the administration imposed in July on goods from **86 countries** at **10% or 12.5%**, covering **99.4% of US imports**. It is the **third time in less than two years** that the courts have been asked to strike down the President's near-global tariff architecture.

The legal sequence the plaintiffs are attacking:

1. **February 2026** — the Supreme Court struck down the April 2025 "reciprocal" tariffs, forcing **more than $100 billion of refunds**.
2. **The same day** — the administration imposed a **worldwide 10% duty under Section 122** of the Trade Act of 1974, which caps that authority at **150 days**. The trade court ruled against it; an appeals court stayed that ruling, allowing the duties to run their statutory course until they expired in July.
3. **July 2026** — the administration replaced them with the **Section 301 forced-labour tariffs**, justified as a response to 86 countries failing to enforce bans on trade in goods produced with forced labour.

The plaintiffs' filing states the case bluntly: *"Defendants have now tried to re-create materially the same global tariff regime under three disparate statutes… there is a reason they did not begin with Section 301 and are instead turning to it now only as a last resort."* They also cite the President's own July remark to Fox News that the 301 duties are **"doing the same thing"** as the tariffs the Supreme Court struck down. The Justice Department argues USTR acted consistently with "the text, object, and purpose of the Section 301 statute."

**Why this is a stock story and not a legal one.** The Section 301 regime is the tariff baseline that the 2026 import-cost structure — holiday orders, retail gross margins, reshoring capex decisions, and the administration's own tariff-revenue estimates — is currently built on. A ruling against it would be the third refund event in under two years and would reopen the durable-tariff question; a ruling for it converts a live risk into a priced one. Note that **India sits in the lower 10% tier** of this regime, which is why the outcome matters to Indian exporters as much as to US importers.

## Trump's July Disclosure Lands: 1,156 Trades, a Strategy Buy at the 2026 Lows, and SpaceX in Both Directions

## The Canada Import Ban Is Live: $1 Billion of Alcohol, Dairy and Motorcycles Barred at 12:01 a.m.

**The United States began banning imports of Canadian alcoholic beverages, dairy products and motorcycles at 12:01 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday, September 29** — roughly **$1 billion** of goods, imposed under **Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930**.

The mechanics matter as much as the headline:

- **Five proclamations** were signed under Section 338. The White House fact sheet confirms the actions respond to Canada's own retaliatory tariffs on about **$20 billion** of US exports (15%, 25% and 50% tiers on steel, dairy and agricultural equipment), which took effect after talks collapsed.
- The **alcohol ban covers most products** — beer, wine, whisky, bourbon, rum, vodka, vermouth, tequila, mezcal and brandy. The dairy ban covers **whey protein, invert molasses, cane molasses and non-alcoholic beer**. Various cheeses were added to the 50% tariff list rather than banned outright, along with certain paper, aluminium, wood, furniture and lighting products.
- The **$50 billion GSA removal** direction stands: USTR and GSA are to strip Canadian-origin products from the Multiple Award Schedules.
- **Section 338 duties apply regardless of USMCA** and **stack on top of Section 232** — so affected goods can face combined rates well above the headline 50%.

**Why the timing matters.** Only **two countries have chosen to retaliate rather than negotiate — China and Canada** — and the US has now escalated against both in the same week. For US consumer-facing importers, the practical effect is narrow ($1 billion against $880 billion of annual two-way trade) but the USMCA renewal is now directly in question. The structure of the response — bans, not tariffs — is also new: a tariff raises the cost of entry, a ban removes the product.

## The US and China Cut Tariffs on $60 Billion — the "30-for-30" Framework

**On September 28 the two governments released reciprocal product lists covering roughly $30 billion each** — $60 billion of goods in total — under a framework the White House calls **"30-for-30"**, days after Xi Jinping's state visit to Washington.

The asymmetry in the lists is the story:

- **China's list covers 1,619 categories of US goods** — agricultural commodities, personal-care products, timber and wood products, **medical equipment**, and **US coal** — with over 90% of covered products moving to "most-favoured-nation" treatment, which in practice erases country-specific tariffs on those lines.
- **The US list covers only 77 categories** of Chinese goods: fireworks, tableware, glass and wooden Christmas ornaments, artificial flowers, children's toys, footballs, car seats and small appliances. This is a consumer-goods list, chosen to show through at retail.
- **The baseline structure is unchanged**: 30% on Chinese goods and 10% on US goods, with the truce **extended to January 10, 2027**. The deal lands *under* a separate **12.5% levy** applied to many Chinese products after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs in February, on top of Section 301 and Section 232 duties.
- **Soybeans were left off China's tariff-cut list** and remain at 10%, but China separately committed to purchasing **25 million metric tons of US soybeans annually through 2028** — a volume promise, not a tariff cut.
- A **US-China Board of Trade** was operationalised, with a working group on agricultural market access. The White House also confirmed a **"US-China Super Intelligence Dialogue"** — both sides agreed to use the term "super intelligence" rather than "artificial intelligence" — with the next exchange due **by November 2026**, plus stated progress on fentanyl precursors and "continued work" on rare-earth and critical-mineral supply-chain shortages.

**The read.** The consumer-goods list is small enough that it will not move retail inflation in either direction, and most of this year's holiday inventory has already shipped. The material facts are (a) the truce extension to January 10 removes a Q4 tail risk, and (b) rare earths are now an explicit agenda item rather than an unresolved threat.

## The Long End Is Still the Story: 30-Year Above 5.6%, Consumer Confidence at a 2014 Low

**The long bond is doing the tightening.** On Tuesday the **30-year Treasury yield climbed above 5.6% intraday — the highest since June 2002** — and the **benchmark 10-year topped 5.29% at its session high**, its highest in 24 years. That is the second leg of a repricing that began before the Fed moved: the 10-year was already above 5% on the eve of the September 16 decision, and touched 5.196% on September 25, which was then the highest since 2007.

The equity tape absorbed it without a break:

- **Dow 51,349.92 (-131.59, -0.26%)**
- **S&P 500 7,670.84 (-12.85, -0.17%)**
- **Nasdaq Composite 26,797.54 (-22.84, -0.09%)**
- **Russell 2000 2,807.92 (-0.4%)**; VIX ~16.04; **gold $4,210.50 (+0.74%)**

All three benchmarks closed off their lows but logged back-to-back losing sessions. **Bank stocks led the decline** — JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Bank of America all fell, with XLF closing lower, which is the tell that this is a rates-and-credit story rather than a growth scare. The S&P 500 and Dow have lost 0.2% and 3.5% in September, with the Dow on course to snap a five-month winning streak.

**Macro backdrop**: the **Conference Board consumer confidence index fell to 81.9 in September, down 6.7 points from August and the lowest since 2014**. US debt has crossed **$40 trillion**; Treasury Secretary Bessent has been running off-schedule buybacks of longer-dated bonds and doubling the original programme to $4 billion to try to pull the long end down. It has not worked.

**September 30 update — the move reversed at the front.** The PCE release covered above pulled short-dated yields lower and lifted US equities in the September 30 session, with the long end easing off its highs. The distinction to hold on to is that the 5.6% print on the 30-year is a **term-premium** story about fiscal supply and inflation risk, and a single soft inflation print does not resolve it — but it does relieve the front end, which is what the October hike odds are priced off, and that is where the market's reaction was concentrated.
