## The 100% Tariff That Never Arrived

India spent September preparing for a tariff that would have applied to **every Indian good entering the United States**, not just the ones containing Russian inputs. Under **Section 113** of the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude or gas were to carry an additional **100% ad valorem** duty. India is one of those five.

On **Friday, October 16**, President Trump signed an [executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/10/implementing-the-lindsey-o-graham-sanctioning-russia-and-iran-act-of-2026/) that set the rate at 100% and then waived it under **Section 115(a)** of the same Act, after certifying to Congress that the waiver was in the national interest. The duties remain written into the order — they are suspended, not repealed, and remain suspended only until the president or the secretary of state decides otherwise.

Two details matter more than the headline:

- **The trigger is prospective.** Section 2(b) of the order applies the 100% only where a country "knowingly makes new purchases of crude oil or natural gas that originated in the Russian Federation **on or after October 18, 2026**." Cargoes bought before that date do not count. The waiver makes the point moot for now, but it is the clause that decides what happens if the waiver falls.
- **It is a waiver with a review date.** Secretary of State **Marco Rubio** said on Saturday that Trump would revisit the decision in **60 days** — putting the next decision point around mid-December, weeks after the midterms and roughly when the India-US bilateral trade agreement is meant to be signed.

Rubio was explicit about the linkage on Monday, speaking in Uzbekistan: "We are trying to finalise a trade deal with India right now and we are very close to doing that. So, that's one of the things we take into consideration when the President makes a decision about national security waivers." **Michael Kugelman** of the Atlantic Council called the pause "the confidence-building measure that gives the two sides the negotiating space to get over the final hump."

The Indian government had warned in September that the new tariff measures could hurt bilateral ties. The energy arithmetic behind that warning is straightforward: India is the **world's third-largest oil importer and consumer**, Russia accounted for **nearly half of India's oil imports** earlier this year, and purchases have been falling since August as refiners tried to stay ahead of the statute.

## Rubio Lands in Mumbai Tomorrow, and the Deal Is One Signature Away

The Secretary of State arrives in India on **Tuesday, October 20** for a two-day visit that includes a meeting with External Affairs Minister **S. Jaishankar** on trade, energy, investment and defence, and stops in **Mumbai and Jamnagar**. The State Department's own framing is "enhanced energy cooperation" and meetings with business leaders on commercial investment.

Jamnagar is not a diplomatic courtesy stop. It is **Reliance's refining complex, roughly 1.4 million barrels per day of capacity**, and it is the physical asset that makes India a swing buyer of crude in a market where the US is trying to control who buys Russian barrels.

The trade file Rubio is arriving to close has been in legal drafting since mid-October. Commerce and Industry Minister **Piyush Goyal** said on October 15 that the **first tranche of the India-US bilateral trade agreement has been finalised** and is going through "the final contours, the legal drafting", to be operationalised once India has a "comparable advantage or competitive advantage over our peer countries" — the reference being Bangladesh, Vietnam and Indonesia. Wheat, rice, maize and soymeal remain excluded, as do sensitive dairy lines.

The one thing the waiver does not touch is the **Section 301 excess-capacity investigation into India**, which Washington has not yet completed and which New Delhi has repeatedly identified as the binding constraint on timing. India joined the US-led structural overcapacity initiative earlier this month, which helps politically but does not close the case.

## The Export Basket Has Its Tail Risk Back, Not a New Advantage

The correction that matters for exporters is a correction of a probability, not a change in the tariff schedule. India's existing US duties — the **10% Section 301 forced-labour tier** plus product-specific measures — are untouched by Friday's order. What has gone is the scenario in which every Indian shipment faced an additional 100% because of where the crude came from.

That is worth separating from the two files that are still live:

- **US anti-dumping and countervailing duty finals on crystalline silicon solar cells from India, Indonesia and Laos.** Commerce's preliminary dumping margin on India was **123.04%**, and final duty orders are scheduled for **October 26, 2026**. Nothing in the Graham Act waiver touches this.
- **Section 232 and Section 301 product coverage**, which continues to expand by product line rather than by country.

So the correct read of Monday is asymmetric: the downside scenario for the whole export basket narrowed, while the specific sector-level risks that were already in the price stayed exactly where they were.

## The Rupee's Seventh Straight Gain Is Doing Half the Work

The currency has become the quiet policy instrument of this rally. The rupee strengthened for a **seventh consecutive session** on Monday, touching **94.80** in early trade before settling near **94.95** — extending Friday's close of **94.96** and a weekly gain of about **1.9%**, the strongest in seven months. That move came after the RBI's October package of forex-derivative curbs, a dollar window for the oil marketing companies and a 20% risk reserve requirement, and it has run alongside persistent foreign selling.

For importers, a stronger rupee is a direct margin input. For exporters — the same basket the waiver just helped — it is a partial offset, which is why the export read should not be treated as linear.
