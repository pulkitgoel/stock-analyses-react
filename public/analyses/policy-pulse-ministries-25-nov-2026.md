The insurance regulator constituted its review committee on Monday evening. The market was shut on Tuesday. Today was the first session that could price it, and it priced it at up to 8% before giving most of it back.

IRDAI's six-member expert committee, chaired by Indu Bhushan — the founding chief executive of Ayushman Bharat — will examine the 2.18 lakh responses it received to its September consultation paper "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution." More than 13,000 participants answered 32 questions by the extended 31 October deadline, against roughly 650 comments a fortnight before it. The other members are Suresh Goyal of NCAER, A.V. Girija Kumar, formerly of Oriental Insurance, Praveen Vashishta, formerly of Howden Insurance Brokers India, and Mahesh Kumar Sharma, formerly of SBI Life, with IRDAI executive director Ayyaj Tamboli as convener. The report is due by 31 December.

The paper itself is the most consequential thing to hit insurance distribution economics in years. It reintroduces product-wise commission caps: term-plan commissions, currently averaging 51% of first-year premium, would fall to 30%; traditional life plans with a ten-year-plus premium term would be capped at 25%; new individual health plans at 20% with renewals at 10%. Banks and brokers would face lower caps than individual agents, gifts and trips would count as commission, agents in towns of 50,000 or fewer would get up to 20% extra, and commission could be clawed back on a mis-sold policy.

A committee that must report by 31 December is not a repeal. It is a delay with a deadline — after this quarter, before the next one — and the intraday-to-close fade is the honest read of how much the market believes in a full rollback.

The dispersion is the information. HDFC Life, which is the most bank-bancassurance-levered large insurer and therefore the most exposed to a cap on bank commissions, held the most of its gain. SBI Life gave back four-fifths of an intraday 5.3%. ICICI Lombard, a general insurer whose motor book sits under the same paper, closed lower. The market is separating the agency-heavy distributors from the bancassurers, which is the opposite of what a blanket cap would imply — a lower bank cap should hurt the bancassurers more. Read the day as relief on the process, not a conclusion on the substance.

PB Fintech is the name with the most explicit disclosure of exposure. Management has said the current proposals could reduce core revenue by roughly 30% before offsetting actions, with the greatest pressure in general insurance, and has flagged slower hiring and reduced marketing as offsets. Jefferies, in a note this week, models a 41% upside case if the norms are moderated. Both of those are analyst and management estimates, not regulator guidance.

## SEBI's quieter day

Two circulars and a speech, none of which moved a stock, all of which matter at the margin.

The first keeps the "significant indices" list unchanged after the January-June 2026 review. BSE 200 and Nifty CPSE stay on it despite failing the ₹20,000 crore average AUM test that governs inclusion. That threshold is not decorative — the tag carries derivative and regulatory consequences for the index — so the regulator chose continuity over its own rule and said so in the circular. When a rule is suspended once, the question for the next review is whether the threshold survives.

The second amends the KYC Master Circular, effective 24 December. Supplementary client information now centralises at the KYC Registration Agency rather than being collected in Part II of the account-opening form; overseas address proof becomes optional for OCI cardholders and foreign nationals resident in India who can show an Indian address; name-update documentation is simplified; and records verified by KRAs against official databases are treated as validated and portable, with attribute-level validation flags.

The third was SEBI chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey at the Dhanam BFSI Summit in Kochi, confirming that the regulator is exploring an AI sandbox and a domestically governed AI model for the securities market, and reviewing the IPO and SME IPO frameworks, corporate bond liquidity and distribution, and the AIF, REIT and InvIT regulations. His line worth keeping: "an alert is not a finding, and an algorithm cannot assume responsibility."

Separately, and undated in the material available to me, SEBI has issued a consultation paper proposing to scrap the additional disclosure requirements imposed on offshore funds in August 2023 — the ones that apply to FPIs with more than 50% of Indian equity concentrated in a single corporate group. Congress's Jairam Ramesh has opposed it, noting the paper follows the prime minister's 19 November FICCI address and citing SEBI's own September 2026 order in the minimum-public-shareholding matter. It is a consultation paper, not a notification.

And then, at 6:41 p.m. IST — three hours after the close — USTR published a determination proposing tariffs of up to 25% on all goods of India, net of existing Section 301 duties, with 17% available if India signs an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade. The Indian market has not traded a single tick on that. Tomorrow's open is its first.

That sequencing matters more than today's close. A 1.85% rally in FMCG, pharma and banks, on a falling oil price and an eight-month-high rupee, is what a market does when it is buying domestic India. The question it has not yet been asked is whether it will pay for that domestic India at a 25% goods-tariff headline.
