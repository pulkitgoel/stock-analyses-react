The escalation moved from the chokepoint to the exporter this week, and the price did what it should. The more informative move was in the assets that usually hedge it — both of them fell.

## First: Brent at $116 and the attacks are inside Saudi Arabia

**Brent's December contract traded at $116.48 late in London on Friday, up $3.31 or 2.92% from Thursday's $113.17 settle.** It had already reached $114.72 by mid-morning; either print is the highest since early May. West Texas Intermediate traded around $100.60.

The geography is the change. Through the summer the risk was transit through the Strait of Hormuz. This week the Houthis claimed responsibility for attacks on **Saudi Aramco's Yanbu port, the Shaybah oil field and King Fahd airport** in the eastern region, and **Aramco told staff to leave its Dhahran headquarters**.

Iran's Revolutionary Guard said it struck **four tankers conducting oil transfers in the northern Gulf and that three other vessels hit mines west of the Strait**, with Iranian state television reporting seven tankers caught fire or damaged. The Associated Press could not verify the claim; if confirmed it would be the most ships damaged in a single day since the war began on February 28. Trump dismissed it directly: Iran is *"making up fake hits, mines, and everything else, because more Oil is coming through the Hormuz Strait now than it did even prior to the beginning of the Conflict."*

**The physical-versus-paper distinction is the falsifiable part of this trade.** Dated Brent — the price of an actual cargo — has come down roughly **$15 in two weeks toward the $120 mark**, while the futures curve has risen. The two converged from opposite directions. That is not the same thing as a fresh supply shock, and it is the reason a single headline can take $6 off the price as fast as it added it.

Flow data supports the same reading. Kpler's seven-day average through Hormuz to October 17 was about **10.2 million barrels a day, 41% below the pre-war baseline of 17.3 million**, but regional flows including Gulf of Oman terminals and the Red Sea were only **19% below** baseline — and the East-West pipeline is back at **7 million barrels a day**, which means Saudi exports no longer need the strait.

The product market is where the damage is concentrated. **US retail diesel averaged $6.32 a gallon on Friday and hit a record, against $3.81 before the war**, with gasoline at **$4.28 from $2.94**, per EIA data for the week to October 19. Diesel futures are $4.96.

## Second: the safe-haven trade inverted, which is the real signal

**On Thursday the 10-year Treasury yield fell 19.3 basis points to 4.952% — the largest one-day decline since March 10, 2023.** The two-year fell 18.8 points to 4.635% and the 30-year 15.1 points to 5.286%. It was the first close below 5% since September 23, from a yield that had approached 5.4% a fortnight earlier.

The stated cause was not the war. It was **AI funding**. Nvidia's Jensen Huang, on CNBC: *"There's a scarcity of capital. The demand for the capital is much greater than the availability of the capital."* Meta fell nearly 8% after reporting its first cash outflow in more than a decade. The Nasdaq lost 2.60% to 26,008.86, its worst day since June 5, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 6.7%.

Rate expectations repriced with it: **the odds of a hike at the October 27-28 FOMC fell to 7.2% from 27.7% a week earlier**, with roughly 76% still priced for December.

Then the part that deserves more attention than the oil paragraph. **Spot gold broke below $4,000, touching about $3,986, now roughly 22% under the January 26 record of $5,110.50, and bitcoin went under $81,000 on Thursday despite more than $1 billion of ETF inflows.** On a day of military escalation, with the VIX up, both classic hedges were sold. That is what raising cash looks like, not what changing your mind looks like — and the destination was Treasuries, which is exactly what the yield move says.

## Third: SpaceX is being priced as a fourth carrier

**Bloomberg reported Friday that SpaceX is in talks with at least one major tower owner — Crown Castle — for access to at least 80,000 US cell sites.** The reaction was immediate and lopsided: **T-Mobile fell 5% to $150.74, AT&T 4% to $21.76, Verizon 3% to $43.70, while Crown Castle rose as much as 12% and American Tower 5.6%.** SpaceX itself added 2.2%.

This is the second step. In early October SpaceX agreed to buy up to 14 MHz of paired 800 MHz spectrum from Grain Management for a reported **$8 billion**, which sent the same three carriers down 10-13% in a session. That deal needs FCC approval and is expected to close in the first half of 2027. Spectrum without towers does not make a network; towers without spectrum do not either. Together they are the outline of a fourth national carrier.

The bear case on the carriers is pricing and churn at the low end. The bull case is physical: **UBS puts SpaceX at 77 MHz against 300-350 MHz for the incumbents, and MoffettNathanson estimates tens of billions of dollars and several years to build a terrestrial network.** Truist's read is that talks are not signed master lease agreements. AT&T and T-Mobile report October 26 and 28, which is where the question gets answered.

Europe closed lower with energy the only strong sector, up about 2.6%: **STOXX 600 around 633.8, down 0.6%; DAX near 25,101, down 1.1%; FTSE 100 near 10,663, down 0.6%; CAC 40 down between 1% and 1.4%.** The single-stock moves were earnings, not macro — **Kering fell more than 11% on weaker Gucci sales, and Volvo Cars more than 27% on a larger-than-expected operating loss and further job cuts.**

In the US at 11:31 am ET the split was stark: **Dow 52,380.07, down 487.91 or 0.92%, against S&P 500 7,655.59, down 0.10%, and Nasdaq 26,075.37, up 0.26%.** The 10-year was back at 4.994%, the VIX at 18.99, December gold at $4,014. The Dow's weakness against a flat S&P is heavily the telecom block — AT&T and Verizon are both Dow components and both fell 3-4% — rather than a second AI leg down.
