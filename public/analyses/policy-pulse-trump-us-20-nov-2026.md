He put a date on the decision, and the date is next year.

President Trump, flying back from Asia on Friday, was asked by Reuters whether new large-scale strikes on Iran were possible. "Well, I'll talk to you about it after the beginning of the year," he said. "We've got time."

That is not a peace signal. Another carrier, the USS Roosevelt, arrived in the Middle East this week, and The New York Times reported on Wednesday that he is weighing "extensive" new strikes, partly on Israeli warnings that Iran is rebuilding its missile and drone arsenals. The Senate rejected a measure to curb the war on Thursday. What changed on Friday is the timing, and timing is what a war premium is priced off.

## The premium coming out

Brent crude traded at $88.04 a barrel at 10:30 a.m. ET against a $91.98 settle on Thursday, down 4.3% on the session. West Texas Intermediate was $81.40 against $83.21. Brent is down roughly 15% in November.

The equity reaction is not clean, and it is worth saying so. ExxonMobil was up 0.88% at $160.33, Chevron 1.04% at $208.65 and ConocoPhillips 1.23% at $136.65, while the services side fell — SLB down 2.24% at $46.83 and Halliburton down 1.65% at $31.20. If the crude move were being read straight through, the majors would be lower too. Treat the energy complex on Friday as a bounce inside a bad month, not as a verdict on the barrel.

The cleaner read-through is on the cost side. Jet fuel is the second-largest cost line at every US carrier and a $10 move in crude is on the order of $4 billion a year of industry fuel bill at current consumption. Delta was up 0.32% at $83.44, United 0.37% at $105.92, Southwest 0.13% at $45.67 and American down 1.45% at $12.53. The airlines are not rallying, because the AI selloff is drowning everything except the Dow. But the input cost has moved their way for a month.

## Seventeen billion dollars, itemised

The other half of the Asia trip is a fact sheet rather than a communiqué, which makes it the more useful document. The White House put the two-day Philippines state visit and the ASEAN summit at more than $17 billion of commercial deals and strategic investments for US companies, and it named parties.

The largest line is aviation. Boeing and GE Aerospace finalised a $12 billion sale of up to 20 787 aircraft with GE engines to Philippine Airlines — 15 firm under Boeing's own announcement, with options on five more — which the White House says supports 47,000 American jobs. Lockheed Martin gets a smaller but more strategically loaded one: Thailand intends to spend up to $123 million on HIMARS, the first such purchase in mainland Southeast Asia, supporting 400 US jobs. Vertiv is to be awarded a data centre contract in Brunei. Astranis is offering connectivity from its AGILA satellite to the Philippines in a deal worth up to $200 million; Astranis is private, so there is no ticker to trade. Subic Grain, also private, will build a $40 million fuel depot at Subic Bay.

The Philippines package also carries more than $2 billion in proposed Foreign Military Financing loans and a $400 million FMF commitment, aimed at long-range fires, command and control, and multi-domain drones. That is a financing line for US defence primes, not a signed contract.

Thailand is the deal with substance. The two sides reached substantial conclusion on a reciprocal agreement that, once signed, eliminates tariffs on 99% of goods and removes non-tariff barriers on American vehicles, medical devices, pharmaceuticals, ethanol and poultry. Thailand committed to an additional $2.6 billion of US agricultural purchases. Signature and entry into force still require domestic procedures on both sides, and that gap is the difference between a headline and a tariff line.

## The instrument that decides whether any of it holds

USTR Jamieson Greer said on Friday that he expects to conclude the pending Section 301 investigation "certainly" by the end of the year, per Bloomberg Law.

That is the operative sentence for anyone holding trade-exposed US equities. The Section 301 apparatus — the forced-labor tier at 10% and 12.5% that replaced Section 122 in July, plus the separate structural excess-capacity probe opened in March against 15 economies — is the durable tariff architecture, and a concluded probe can add a second layer without a new statute. The same instrument is what the India bilateral has to clear: Washington promised New Delhi lighter duties than its competitors, and a year-end 301 conclusion is the deadline that promise has to survive.

Canada is the live case. Trade minister Dominic LeBlanc said Ottawa is seeking an interim agreement by the end of the year and wants the Section 338 tariffs Trump imposed in September removed entirely, while conceding that some sectoral duties will remain. Trump has threatened higher levies on Canadian autos, car parts and steel from January 1 if no deal is reached. Section 338 is the 1930 statute behind the 50% Canadian package. The January date is the one that matters for General Motors at $81.39, Ford at $11.52 and Stellantis at $3.47.

## What to watch

- **Noon ET Friday** — the White House press conference. Trump has framed it around the Asia trade deals. Watch for a signed instrument rather than an announcement; the Thailand agreement still needs domestic procedures on both sides.
- **Before year end** — Greer's Section 301 conclusion. This is the deadline that decides whether the 10%/12.5% forced-labor tier becomes a base and whether the excess-capacity probe produces a second layer.
- **January 1, 2027** — the threatened increase on Canadian autos, car parts and steel, unless an interim agreement is reached.
- **January 9, 2027** — the 178 Section 301 China exclusions extended on 6 November expire. The US-China truce expires on 10 January, the next day.
- **8-9 December** — FOMC. The 10-year has slipped back below 5% on safe-haven demand, which is the one input that could change the equity picture faster than any trade headline.
- **Iran** — the operative phrase is "after the beginning of the year." A decision inside the first quarter of 2027 collides with a Democratic Congress, which is the constraint Trump did not have in 2025.
