Nvidia beat on every line, raised guidance, and announced a $150 billion buyback. The stock opened 2.4% higher and was down 1.1% an hour later.

That is the whole Global story today, and it is a more useful signal than the numbers.

## The quarter was never the question

Third quarter, ended 25 October: revenue $115.8 billion, up 20% sequentially and 103% year on year, against a $109.15 billion consensus. Data centre contributed $107.6 billion against $101.85 billion expected, edge computing $8.2 billion against $7.49 billion. Non-GAAP gross margin 74.9%, down a tenth of a point sequentially. Non-GAAP earnings per diluted share $2.68 against $2.47.

Guidance for the January quarter is $127.4 billion to $132.6 billion — above the $125.25 billion consensus, and it includes an additional week. Management pointed to roughly 75% revenue growth in fiscal 2028, up from the approximately 70% it discussed three months ago. It cited a $2.5 trillion cloud backlog and said next-generation chips remain on schedule. The company returned $28.1 billion in the quarter and will pay a $0.25 dividend on 23 December to holders of record on 2 December.

The board also said it will not reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive. Huang resigned as president, chief executive and director on 12 November amid a board review following reports about his personal conduct. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive.

So the numbers answered the demand question emphatically and the leadership question not at all. KeyBanc kept the stock at Sector Weight on exactly that basis: uncertainty over the successor keeps it range-bound. Morgan Stanley maintained Buy but cut its target to $267 from $300, arguing that the interim title makes it harder to secure customer commitments and large investments, and that appointing a permanent chief executive should be a priority.

## The China line is going to zero by choice

The most consequential sentence of the week came on the call, not in the release. Kress said Nvidia is not currently shipping Hopper-generation data-centre products to China — the most advanced products the US government licenses it to sell there. It sold $2.4 billion of them in the previous quarter.

Set that against the policy calendar. On 16 November the Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim rule requiring overseas data centres serving customers in China, Russia, Iran or Cuba to identify end users, and imposing case-by-case licensing on exports to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. On 19 November Trump, Xi and Putin meet in Shenzhen.

A company voluntarily zeroing a licensed revenue line in the week of a summit is not a supply problem. It is a political one, and it tells you management expects the export regime to tighten rather than loosen. The $2.4 billion is roughly 2% of quarterly revenue — absorbable, which is precisely why it can be given away as a gesture.

## Shenzhen: three leaders, one photograph

Xi Jinping opened APEC in Shenzhen with a pitch for "free and open trade," positioning Beijing as a provider of a "stable, predictable and beneficial environment for global enterprises," and taking aim at "unilateralism and protectionism." He called for partnerships in scientific and technological innovation, deeper cooperation in the digital economy and artificial intelligence, and a green and low-carbon transition.

Vladimir Putin, speaking at the same CEO summit, said the sanctions imposed on Russia had backfired and strengthened it, and that he expects "big potential benefit" from the trilateral meeting on Thursday: "At a time when military-political risks are on the rise, what is desperately needed is unity rather than division, and dialogue rather than ultimatums." He and Xi held bilateral talks on Wednesday, their fifth meeting this year.

Trump arrived in Shenzhen on Wednesday and was received by Xi at the airport; they embraced on the tarmac. It is their third meeting this year. Taiwan is expected to be raised. Trump said he is eyeing one-on-one talks with Putin and with Kim Jong Un, which Secretary of State Marco Rubio declined to confirm.

Two things to hold onto. First, Washington had signalled that Ukraine was not expected to be addressed, and then Trump said it would be — the agenda is being set in public and revised in public, which means the communiqué is not the tradeable artefact. Second, the rare-earth clock: Beijing extended its pause on wider export controls by two months to 10 January, one day before the US-China tariff truce expires. Watching whether that gets extended again is worth more than any statement issued on Thursday.

## Anthropic prices tonight, above its range

The other half of the AI trade sets its price this evening, and it is being marked up rather than down.

Anthropic is expected to price on 18 November and begin trading on Nasdaq as ANT on 19 November: 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78, comprising 801.4 million primary and 23.65 million secondary, with an over-allotment of up to 123.75 million. At the top of the range that is $64.4 billion, plus a $26 billion private placement led by Alphabet and Nvidia at $10 billion each among seven strategic partners — $90.4 billion in total, above SpaceX's $75 billion base offering.

The Wall Street Journal reports the company expects to price above the range, with advisers discussing numbers that would value it at around $1.5 trillion to $1.6 trillion against roughly $1.33 trillion at the top of the filed range. The order book was covered on the first day of the roadshow and is now close to seven times.

The operating numbers behind it are not speculative. Revenue was $33.24 billion in the first nine months of 2026, against $2.5 billion a year earlier; the third quarter alone was $16.96 billion, up 1,127%; the annualised run rate reached about $81 billion on 31 October. Against that sit $895.4 billion of committed spend, mostly compute and infrastructure, of which $447.5 billion falls due before 31 December 2030 and $69.8 billion within twelve months. Cash and short-term investments were $131.9 billion at 30 September.

A seven-times-covered book is a statement about allocation scarcity, not about value. The 2026 IPO cohort is trading roughly 20% below its offer prices, according to the Financial Times. Note the float: the shares on offer are about 6% of the company, so index weight on day one is small or zero depending on the provider, and it climbs only as lock-ups expire.

## India: the reverse-AI trade has reversed

The spillover into India is no longer what the pattern said three months ago, and this is the part most likely to be acted on wrongly.

When chip stocks crashed in July on China-competition fears, capital rotated out of semiconductors and into IT services — Nifty IT rose 3.75% on a day the KOSPI fell 10.8%. That trade is over. Nifty IT fell 0.9% on Tuesday with HCL Technologies down 2.09% to ₹1,355.70 and Infosys down 1.32%, and IT was again among the worst sectors on Wednesday alongside consumer durables, metals and auto.

The money that arrived is buying the opposite. Foreign portfolio investors have put a record $10 billion into Indian equities in November — a six-year monthly high — after ₹70,704 crore of selling in September and October, and benchmarks are up 9.4% (Nifty) and 10.2% (Sensex) month to date. It is going into realty and PSU banks, the domestic cyclicals, while Indian IT is sold. Domestic institutions are on the other side of that bid, having sold ₹3,519.05 crore on 16 November.

So the Indian expression of an Nvidia beat is not Infosys. It is a stronger rupee and a foreign bid for domestic demand, with IT as the funding source.
