## 2. Business Overview

Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders is the only public-sector Indian shipyard that builds both destroyers and conventional submarines for the Indian Navy. Its dockyard at Mazagon, Mumbai, traces to 1774, the company was incorporated in 1934, and the Government of India took control in 1960. It was listed in 2020 to an oversubscription of roughly 157 times and was granted Navratna status in 2025.

**Two reporting segments**

| Segment | What it does |
|---------|--------------|
| Shipbuilding | Destroyers, frigates, corvettes, offshore patrol vessels, fast patrol vessels, plus commercial vessels (multipurpose hybrid vessels, platform supply vessels, dredgers, tugs) |
| Submarine and Heavy Engineering | Conventional submarines, submarine medium refit and life certification, air-independent propulsion, offshore platforms for ONGC |

**What makes it structurally different.** Only one other Indian yard builds warships at this scale, and none in the private sector builds conventional submarines. The moat is not brand — it is the combination of a submarine-capable yard, a trained workforce, naval certification and existing programme relationships, none of which can be bought or built quickly. Capacity is stated at 11 submarines and 10 warships concurrently.

**Execution record.** 809 vessels delivered since 1960. All four P15B destroyers were delivered ahead of schedule, and seven destroyers were delivered between 2014 and 2025 — roughly one every 18 months. The four P17A Nilgiri-class stealth frigates were delivered between December 2024 and April 2026, the last of them, INS Mahendragiri, on 30 April 2026 with 75% indigenisation. All six Kalvari-class Scorpene submarines were delivered between 2017 and 2025. This is a company that finishes what it signs.

**Customers.** Predominantly the Ministry of Defence (Indian Navy), with the Indian Coast Guard, ONGC and a small commercial book including an export order from Navi Merchants A/S.

| Net Cash Flow | 1,148 | 1,655 | 67 | **−2,419** |
| Free Cash Flow | 1,401 | 347 | 1,298 | **−2,828** |
| CFO/OP | 228% | 96% | 145% | **−74%** |

**This is the most important number in the report and it needs explaining rather than condemning.** Operating cash flow swung from +₹2,102 Cr in FY25 to −₹2,654 Cr in FY26 while reported profit *rose* 6.8%. Free cash flow was −₹2,828 Cr. On its own, that looks like a serious deterioration in earnings quality.

The mechanism is the business model. Warship and submarine contracts are paid in advance. Those advances sit in "Other Liabilities" as a customer-advance liability, which is why that line is enormous (₹17,272 Cr in FY26 against ₹10,738 Cr of annual expenses). The same line fell ₹3,572 Cr during FY26 — from ₹20,844 Cr to ₹17,272 Cr — as ships were delivered and the advances were converted into revenue. In accounting terms, cash that arrived in earlier years was recognised as revenue in FY26 while the offsetting liability unwound; the cash movement is real but it belongs to delivery, not to deterioration in the underlying business.

**Two genuine caveats, though.** First, this mechanism works in reverse as well: if new orders are not signed, no fresh advances arrive, and the lumpy negative operating cash flow does not self-correct with the same revenue. Second, the years in which MDL was booking large advances (FY23: CFO/OP 228%) look flattering in exactly the way FY26 looks ugly. The honest reading is that operating cash flow here is driven by contract timing, and the correct companion question is not "was FY26 bad?" but "how much advance is arriving now?" — and the order book answer is: not much.

**The order book has fallen about 53% from its FY24 peak.** That is not an accounting artefact — it is what happens when a yard successfully finishes its programmes.

Read the "units pending" column carefully, because it is the whole story. The two largest programmes by contract value — P15B destroyers (₹28,753 Cr) and P75 Kalvari submarines (₹29,981 Cr) — have **zero units pending**. P17A frigates, at ₹27,257 Cr, have zero pending. Those three programmes alone represent ₹85,991 Cr of contracts that have been substantially executed. What remains is mostly smaller coast-guard and commercial work, plus ONGC and AIP.

**Why this matters more here than at a normal company.** Shipbuilding revenue is recognised on progress toward delivery. When the yard has a full book, revenue is visible years out. When the book empties, there is nothing to recognise — and the gap cannot be filled quickly, because a warship takes years from signature to revenue.

### The replacement pipeline, and the catch

Two large programmes are expected:

- **Project 75(I)** — approximately **₹99,000 Cr** for six conventional submarines with the Indian Navy. Contract Negotiation Committee discussions are complete; the contract awaits Government approval. When first reported in March 2026 the stock jumped about 8% to ₹2,352, because the contract value exceeded the company's market capitalisation at the time. Revenue would spread over six or more years.
- **Three additional P75(I) / Kalvari-class submarines** — reported in the ₹30,000-40,000 Cr range.

Management has stated the order book could touch ₹1 lakh crore for FY27 if these land. In FY26 management expected the order book to cross ₹1.25 lakh crore on the same two orders, and that did not happen — the book closed at ₹20,535 Cr instead.

**And here is the sentence that matters most, from management itself: revenue recognition on newly signed contracts begins roughly two years after signing.** Even a FY27 signature on P75(I) therefore contributes almost nothing to FY27 or FY28 revenue. Signing the order fixes the long-term story; it does not fix the next four-to-six quarters.

**The honest conclusion.** India's naval modernisation is real — a 160-ship expansion target, an estimated ₹2.35 lakh crore of shipbuilding spend over the next decade, and MDL's selection as an anchor shipyard for the Dighi and Dugarajapatnam clusters. But between the completion of P17A/P15B/P75 and the arrival of the next generation, MDL faces a period in which revenue has to be carried by a much smaller book. Management is guiding 15-17% revenue growth for FY27; that guidance is currently backed by ₹18,218 Cr of order book against ₹13,323 Cr of trailing revenue, which is roughly 1.4 years of cover. For a business with multi-year build cycles, that is thin visibility.

## 8. Management Commentary (Q1 FY27 Call, July 2026)

**On guidance:**
- FY27 revenue growth guidance: **15-17%**
- Order book target: **₹1 lakh crore for FY27**, contingent on signing the two large programmes
- PBT margin guidance: around **15%**
- Management expects to finalise two mega-contracts by FY27: Project 75I and the three additional P75(I) submarines

**On the mechanics of new orders (the key caveat):**
> Revenue recognition or booking will happen only after two years down the line, because after signing the contract it will take about two years to realise the value of production on ground.

**On seasonality:**
> The next couple of quarters we would be on a path of recovery because our projects are seasonal — during monsoon we are unable to execute offshore. So once the monsoon period is over, the next three quarters we expect that pick-up.

**On the order book target — management's own caution, which is worth keeping in mind against the promotional framing:**
> If everything comes of course it will touch that figure, but at this point in time I won't be able to commit that this is going to happen.

**Tone assessment.** This is a management that has been consistently conservative about the order book and explicit about the timing mechanics — the two-year recognition lag was volunteered, not extracted. That is a point in its favour on disclosure. Against it, the gap between what was promised (₹1.25 lakh crore by FY26) and what arrived (₹20,535 Cr) has now happened once and is being re-promised for FY27, which is exactly the pattern that de-rated the stock.

**Next data point:** Q2 FY27 results on **11 November 2026**. Trading window closed from 1 October 2026. The things to watch are (a) whether revenue growth lands near the 15-17% guidance, (b) whether any order has been signed, and (c) the order book number itself.

A price rise on falling volume with low delivery is a bounce without conviction, and it is the one reading here that argues against the accumulation thesis.
4. **Volume is otherwise unremarkable.** Every session sits between 364K and 883K against a 30-day average of 589K. There is no capitulation spike and no sustained accumulation surge.

**How to weigh this.** Genuine buying interest appeared at ₹1,990-2,010 and it was real, not noise. That is the single most constructive data point in this report and it is why the outlook is "caution" rather than "avoid". But two days of high-delivery buying inside a strong downtrend is a floor being tested by patient money, not a reversal — and the rally immediately after those days came on the weakest delivery of the period, which suggests the buyers did not follow through at higher prices.

## 15. Bull vs Bear Case

### Bull case

1. **India's only submarine-and-destroyer yard.** No private-sector company builds conventional submarines in India. The moat is structural and cannot be replicated quickly, and capacity stands at 11 submarines and 10 warships concurrently.
2. **The cheapest quality in the sector.** 28.6x earnings against a peer median of 44.2x, with the highest ROCE in the group at 36.0% and the only meaningful positive quarterly profit growth. Cochin Shipyard trades at 54.8x for a 14.5% ROCE and declining profit.
3. **The pipeline is genuinely enormous.** P75(I) is ~₹99,000 Cr — larger than the company's market capitalisation when first reported — with six submarines, plus three additional boats and the next-generation destroyer programme. A 160-ship naval expansion and an estimated ₹2.35 lakh crore of shipbuilding spend over the decade sit behind it.
4. **Pristine balance sheet.** Debt-to-equity of 0.05, ₹447 Cr of borrowings against ₹9,755 Cr of net worth, and a track record of paying 28-29% of profits out as dividends for five straight years.
5. **Execution credibility.** All four P15B destroyers delivered ahead of schedule; seven destroyers in 11 years; all four P17A frigates delivered between December 2024 and April 2026; all six Kalvari submarines delivered 2017-2025. This management delivers what it signs.
6. **Management has been transparent about the timing.** The two-year revenue lag from signature to recognition was volunteered, not extracted. That is honest disclosure, and it means the market is not being misled about the shape of the next two years.
7. **Structural positioning beyond warships.** Selected as an anchor shipyard for the Dighi and Dugarajapatnam clusters, a commercial book including export orders from Navi Merchants, and a growing ship-repair and refit annuity.
8. **The stock has already corrected 30% in a year** and sits 20% below the price at which institutions bought the government's stake. A lot of the disappointment is in the price.

### Bear case

1. **The order book has halved and nothing has replaced it.** ₹38,561 Cr in FY24 to ₹18,218 Cr in June 2026 — a 52.8% decline — because the programmes that made up the book have been delivered. Cover is 1.19-1.37 years of revenue against multi-year build cycles.
2. **The revenue recognition lag is brutal for the next two years.** Management: revenue from a new contract begins roughly two years after signing. Even a FY27 P75(I) signature contributes almost nothing before FY29.
3. **Management has missed this exact guidance once already.** They guided an order book above ₹1.25 lakh crore for FY26 contingent on two orders, and closed FY26 at ₹20,535 Cr. The FY27 ₹1 lakh crore target is contingent on the same two orders.
4. **The sell side is cutting, not upgrading.** HSBC initiated at Sell with ₹1,860 in September; Kotak at Sell with ₹1,950 in July citing a two-year air pocket; JPMorgan has been Sell since 2025. The three most recent notes are all negative.
5. **Earnings quality depends on treasury income.** Other income is ~35% of PBT and is included in the company's stated EBITDA and margin guidance. It shrinks when advances shrink — which is precisely what a thin order book causes.
6. **Contingent liabilities of ₹28,181 Cr exceed total assets.** A guarantee or performance-obligation problem on one large programme would be materially worse than the reported balance sheet implies.
7. **Institutions are distributing into a broadening retail register.** DIIs from 5.65% to 5.02%, FIIs from 2.57% to 1.72%, shareholder count from 6.77 lakh to 8.06 lakh.
8. **The stock is expensive on assets.** 8.38x book means there is no valuation floor if the 29% ROE compresses. A DCF-style fair value of ₹901 has been published, and the price is more than double it.
9. **Technically it is broken.** Strong Sell moving averages, price under all major averages but Hull MA(9), ADX 47.8 measuring a downtrend with conviction, and a 52-week low set on 9 October with the stock closing 2% above it.
10. **Quarterly momentum is deteriorating.** Revenue ₹3,850 Cr → ₹2,943 Cr and operating profit ₹543 Cr → ₹447 Cr over the last two quarters, with the June quarter's 12.1% growth running below the 15-17% full-year guidance.

## 16. Final Verdict

### CAUTION — Priced for orders not yet signed

Mazagon Dock is a high-quality business in an unavoidable short-term hole. The franchise is genuinely irreplaceable inside India, the balance sheet is pristine, the execution record is excellent, and at 28.6x earnings it is cheaper than every listed shipbuilding peer while earning better returns. None of that is in dispute.

What is in dispute is what the company will build next, and when it will be paid for it. The order book has fallen 52.8% from its FY24 peak to ₹18,218 Cr because MDL successfully completed P15B, P17A and P75 Kalvari — success creating the current problem. The programmes that would replace them, chiefly the ~₹99,000 Cr P75(I) submarine contract, remain unsigned pending Government approval, and management itself says revenue from a newly signed contract begins about two years later. That combination — a thin book now, and a two-year lag whenever the new orders arrive — means the next four to six quarters have to be carried by a much smaller backlog, while the market is currently paying 8.38x book for a company whose 29% ROE depends on a full yard.

The stock is not expensive against its peers on earnings. It is expensive against the growth that is actually contracted, and the sell side has noticed: the two most recent pieces of coverage are Sells at or below the current price, and Kotak's "two-year revenue air pocket" is a fair description of the arithmetic.

**A word on what would make this a buy, because the case is not dead — it is unproven.** The P75(I) award would transform the long-term picture and is a question of when, not whether, for India's only conventional submarine yard. But a signature is not a revenue event, and the market has already paid for this story once and been disappointed. The disciplined position is to wait for evidence rather than to anticipate it.

### Recommended approach

- **Existing holders:** holding is defensible on the franchise and the balance sheet, but this is not a position to average into while the trend is broken and the order book is falling. The 52-week low at ₹1,987.70 is the level that matters; a decisive break of it with volume would suggest the market is repricing toward the air-pocket scenario.
- **New entrants:** wait. The technical setup is a strong downtrend with oversold conditions — which produces bounces, not bases. There is no reason to own the uncertainty when the next hard data point is three weeks away.
- **What to wait for, specifically:** a signed P75(I) contract, or an order book that stops falling, or Q2 FY27 revenue growth near the guided 15-17%. Any one of those changes the arithmetic; two of them change the thesis.
- **Do not buy the oversold reading.** RSI 29.6 with ADX 47.8 is a strong downtrend, and the momentum Buys on the oscillator panel are mean-reversion signals in a chart that remains below every meaningful average.

### What would change this view

1. **P75(I) signature** — the single largest catalyst. Watch for Government approval and the formal contract.
2. **Order book turning up** in a quarterly disclosure, even marginally.
3. **Q2 FY27 results on 11 November 2026** — revenue growth at or above 15%, and any commentary on the new-order timeline.
4. **A weekly close back above the 20-day average (₹2,150)** with volume above the 20-day average, which would be the first sign the downtrend has ended.
5. **Institutional accumulation returning** — DII or FII holding rising in a monthly disclosure, reversing the current trend.

### Suitable investor profile

Suited to patient investors with a 3-5 year horizon who specifically want exposure to India's naval shipbuilding programme and can tolerate a multi-quarter revenue gap and further drawdown. Not suited to anyone seeking near-term momentum, income (a 0.89% yield), or a valuation floor on asset support — and not suited to a swing trade on the current chart.
