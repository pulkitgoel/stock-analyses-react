India extended its winning run to an eighth full session today. Korea's problem is not that it fell on Friday; it is that the thing which would fix it is a decision Washington has not taken yet.

## The Indian close was the quiet one

**Sensex 81,106.65 (+107.25, +0.13%). Nifty 50 25,044.65 (+58.25, +0.23%).** A session that held the level rather than extending the move.

Read the point changes carefully, because they are not measured against Friday. Both are against Sunday's one-hour Muhurat close: 80,999.40 and 24,986.40, the previous closes implied by the exchange's own arithmetic. Against Friday's 81,028.02 and 25,030.35 the market is roughly flat, and the Muhurat hour itself closed lower. This is the eighth consecutive full session of gains, not the eighth consecutive session — the distinction matters to anyone reconstructing the run.

Breadth was better than the headline. **Nifty Midcap 100 +0.55%, Nifty Smallcap 100 +0.10%**, so the advance was not confined to the index heavyweights. **Nifty PSU Bank rose over 3%** with State Bank of India up 2.31% to ₹1,042, the top Nifty 50 gainer. Pharma and healthcare outperformed: **Divi's Laboratories rose 12.06% to ₹11,565.50** after Q2 net profit grew 64% to ₹1,127 crore on revenue of ₹3,676 crore and an EBITDA margin of 42.6% — a record ₹11,824 intraday, ₹3 trillion of market value for the first time, and a twelfth straight gain — while **Laurus Labs rose 4.87% to ₹2,454**, also a record intraday high. **Nifty IT fell the most** of the sectoral indices: HCL Tech closed 2.29% lower at ₹1,407.

Flows did the work: **FIIs net bought about ₹7,212 crore** against **DII net sales of about ₹7,172 crore**, on NSE provisional figures. The **rupee closed at 93.93 provisional** — flat to Friday's 93.91, which breaks a six-day winning streak rather than extending it. **Brent settled around $97.46, down 1.04%.**

That combination is the whole Indian case in one line: cheaper crude, a stable currency, and foreign money that has stopped leaving. None of it required an Indian policy decision.

## Korea's problem is a policy question, not a cycle question

**KOSPI closed Friday at 5,059.69, down 324.11 points or 6.02%.** The mechanics matter more than the percentage: a **20-minute circuit breaker halted trading from 9:03 a.m** — the tenth activation this year — after the index fell more than 8% intraday into the 4,900s. **Samsung Electronics -7.99%, SK Hynix -9.97%.** Foreigners were **net sellers of roughly 2.74 trillion won, a sixth consecutive session**, taking the six-day total to about **11.99 trillion won**, and 669 KOSPI and KOSDAQ names closed at yearly lows.

The analysts' argument, reported by the Korea Times, is not that chip demand has fallen. It is that Korea's growth has become chip-reliant enough that 2027 GDP is now a function of whether the United States curbs AI data-centre construction. On Bank of Korea data the country ran a record monthly current-account surplus of **$61.5 billion in September**, with semiconductor shipments up roughly 259% year on year — a surplus that is also an exposure.

Forecasts for 2027 have already fanned out: **2.2% from the Korea Development Institute and the finance ministry, 2.9% from the Bank of Korea, 2.8% from the IMF and 2.6% from the OECD.** The BOK updates later this month and the government writes next year's policy plan around it. Finance Minister Lee Hyoung-il promised measures to reduce market volatility and described the fundamentals as "more solid than ever", which is what a finance minister says when the volatility is the story.

The asymmetry is worth naming plainly. A country that exported its way into an AI surplus is now short a policy put: the risk is not that customers stop buying memory, it is that a government decides buildings are the problem.

## The fault line moved inside the sector

Sunday's Nvidia announcement did something unusual to semiconductor pricing: it separated the company from the sector.

The board opened an independent investigation, overseen by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel, after reports that founder and CEO Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort — reports concerning Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman and former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, which US authorities have examined since 2024 over restricted hardware reaching China. The board states it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised". Huang's memo to employees acknowledges he was unfaithful to his wife and supports the review.

Today's tape reads it as a single-name event. **NVDA $179.60, -3.77%** — the only mega-cap chip lower — while **AMD +9.6%, Broadcom +7.9%, TSMC +4.5%, Marvell +7.4%, Arm +5.0%**. The rotation is to the second source, not out of the trade.

The policy half of the same story is the part that does not get a headline. On 4 November the Financial Times reported that Oracle cancelled a **$7 billion, five-year AI-chip lease with Tencent** — up to 100,000 advanced accelerators from South-East Asian data centres — after US officials raised concerns, and that Nvidia has told cloud customers at least **51% of capacity on new contracts** must go to non-Chinese buyers. Reuters, via the Korea Herald, notes that no fresh US regulatory curb has been announced: the tightening is running through pressure on counterparties, which is why it lands in compliance budgets rather than in guidance.

Korean memory makers are the exposed link, because high-bandwidth memory sits inside the accelerator being scrutinised. The compliance cost arrives before any rule does, and it arrives at the two companies whose index weight just did the damage.

## Two more trade fronts, both dated today

- The US published **anti-dumping orders on solar cells from India, Indonesia and Laos**, applicable 9 November: a 123.04% dumping margin for all Indian producers, a 107.17% cash deposit rate and a 126.09% countervailing rate — roughly 249% combined, stacking on existing floors. Indonesia and Laos face similar treatment.
- **Tomorrow, 10 November**, China's suspension of its October 2025 extraterritorial rare-earth export controls expires. The US-China truce runs to 10 January 2027 and the Section 301 exclusions to 9 January, so the next eight weeks are the window in which either side can walk.

## The honest caveat on India

India's outperformance today is real and it is not a moat. It rose because it owns no memory, because crude fell, and because foreign flows returned. If Brent turns back up, the rupee follows the rate differential rather than the oil price, and the same foreign money that bought ₹7,212 crore today has spent most of this year selling. The eighth straight gain is a flow story with a good week behind it, not a re-rating with a thesis in front of it.
