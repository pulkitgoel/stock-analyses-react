## The G7's 100-Million-Barrel Release Meets a Splitting Oil Market

**The Group of Seven agreed on October 2 to a coordinated release of 100 million barrels from emergency reserves through the International Energy Agency**, to begin immediately and run over four months, including a **frontloaded "substantial" diesel release within the first 20 days**. The joint statement also committed members to **"refrain from export restrictions on energy and energy products" against one another**, and asked the IEA to monitor immediate and full implementation of the March 2026 commitments, with a further convening "in the coming days to discuss the possibility of additional diesel releases as necessary."

The trigger was a threat, not a shortfall. **President Trump had threatened to ban US diesel exports** unless European countries put more of their own stock on the market; Treasury Secretary **Scott Bessent** had argued US farmers, truckers and businesses "should not be left carrying the burden" as prices soared. **US retail diesel had reached nearly $6.50 a gallon.** French President **Emmanuel Macron**, whose country holds the G7 presidency, convened the leaders' call; Trump's response on social media: "Europe has just agreed to release a massive amount of their heavily stocked Diesel Oil. The process will begin immediately."

Two qualifiers the market should carry. First, the scale is smaller than it sounds: the IEA statement noted that **around 325 million barrels of the March 11 collective action have already been released — over 80% of the 400 million originally pledged**. Second, stockpile releases buy time rather than fix flows. **Aramco CEO Amin Nasser** said releasing reserves will help economies but will not resolve supply-demand imbalances, and that even after Hormuz reopens it may take **as long as two years** for consuming countries to replenish.

**Monday's tape split by benchmark, which is the most informative feature of this market.** Brent fell about **0.7-0.8% to roughly $101.5-102.3**, WTI fell **1.2-1.9% to $89.3-90.3**, and **Murban traded at $110.80** — a seaborne premium that pushed the **Brent-WTI spread above $11 a barrel**. Analysts attribute the gap to the greater exposure of internationally traded crude to maritime disruption, compounded by **tanker shortages, higher insurance costs and longer voyages, with VLCC freight rates surging**. Two offsetting weekend events: **Houthi missile strikes on Aramco sites near Riyadh**, and **Saudi Aramco's surprise cut to its November official selling price for Arab Light to Asia to a six-year low** — an explicit market-share move that dominated the geopolitical headline. An **OPEC+ subgroup agreed on Sunday to hold November production quotas unchanged.**

The physical supply picture is normalising even as the politics does not. **Gulf crude exports returned to pre-war levels in September**, with producers increasingly relying on alternative routes and ship-to-ship transfers to bypass Hormuz, though **Iranian exports remain below the pre-war average**. Iran says the **Strait of Hormuz will not fully reopen until Washington meets seven conditions**, and **Iran's oil minister has resigned** and been replaced by a top executive amid the US blockade on Tehran's exports, with the rial collapsing as the country runs out of sellable crude and cannot collect payment from customers.

## The Russia Secondary-Sanctions Clock: October 18, and India's Refining Exposure

**US Energy Secretary Chris Wright said on October 5 that the administration does not rule out a decision to impose secondary sanctions on Russia's trade partners by October 18.** Asked whether President Trump would put tariffs on Russia's trade partners by that date under the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026**, Wright said: "Look, he leaves every decision till the end. He'll evaluate the trade-offs."

The statute is unusually blunt. Signed on **September 18, 2026** (House 262-159 on September 16; Senate 86-11 on August 7), it **requires** the President to impose tariffs of up to **100% on all goods imported from countries that are among the top five importers of Russian crude oil or natural gas, or among the top five facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion.** Three features make it the most consequential trade instrument available this month:

- **The tariffs stack on top of all other existing duties** — they are additive to the forced-labour Section 301 regime and any Section 232 measures.
- **Duties on Russian-origin goods can rise up to 500%.**
- **The trigger is a ranking, not conduct.** Designation as a top-five importer is a mechanical test, and India's crude import slate has placed it squarely within that group since 2022.

> **The India-specific read:** this is the single largest tariff shock available to Indian exporters this month, and it is discretionary, unilateral and scheduled to resolve within two weeks. A 100% stacked tariff on Indian goods entering the US would be an order of magnitude beyond anything the interim BTA negotiation has contemplated.

## Critical Minerals: A US-Ukraine Fund's First Deal, and China's Rare-Earth Leverage Extended

**The United States-Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund has closed its first critical-minerals transaction**, a joint investment platform with Ukrainian firm **BGV Group Management** to develop early-stage mining projects. The minerals deal is worth around **$30 million**, part of a broader board-approved package of roughly **$70 million** covering four additional initiatives, including energy projects aimed at winter resilience and industrial battery storage and municipal heating hubs. The fund targets **lithium, titanium, graphite, uranium, oil and natural gas**; ownership of Ukraine's resources remains with Ukraine, and the fund receives a portion of revenues from new licences and royalty payments. The structure was established under the April 2025 minerals agreement, and the fund is actively seeking additional capital.

The reason this matters is the Chinese side of the ledger. As of late September:
- **China's rare-earth export-control suspension has been extended to January 10, 2027**, following the Washington Trump-Xi summit — with **no jointly documented settlement on critical minerals**.
- Beijing reportedly **held export licences as diplomatic leverage** ahead of the summit and is reported to be **eyeballing a stake in MP Materials**.
- Two January 2027 clocks are now converging: the White House target to **block all Chinese rare-earth imports**, and the Pentagon's **DFARS cutoff for Chinese-sourced magnets** in defence procurement.
- Greer said the administration treats the next two months as a **"compliance period"**, and the **US-China Board of Trade recommended each country identify $30 billion of non-sensitive goods** eligible for more favourable tariff treatment.

The Western capital response has shifted decisively from upstream mining to **processing, separation and recycling** — the layer where China's leverage actually lives. The **US Export-Import Bank issued a letter of interest for up to $750 million** for **Aclara's Project Dynamo** rare-earth separation, metals and alloys facility at the Port of Vinton, Louisiana; a **$100 million magnet-recycling joint venture** was formed in Missouri; **Cyclic Materials opened the first commercial-scale rare-earth recycling plant in the US** in Arizona; and the **US-Argentina Andes-Atlantic Corridor commands up to $7 billion of EXIM backing**. The binding constraint identified this month is not capital or geology but **human capital**: North America's rare-earth engineering degree output is less than half the DOE-estimated requirement of roughly **600 per year**, and about a fifteenth of China's.

## Steel, Overcapacity and the G20's Missing Communique

**The G20 trade ministerial in Milwaukee closed without a unified declaration**, which is the outcome the US hosts were content with. The substantive artefact is the endorsed **"Milwaukee Framework"** on steel, which calls on countries to curb market-distorting subsidies, step up monitoring of steel imports and trade circumvention, and endorses **"evidence-based actions"** against excess capacity — **including possibly tariffs**. Transshipment monitoring is the provision that matters most to Asian exporters routing through third countries.

Greer's four themes, as delivered: eliminating forced labour, **"updating" the MFN principle**, denouncing the weaponisation of food trade, and addressing **structural excess capacity**. The political read from Bloomberg Law is that the US went to Milwaukee touting a project to remake the global trading system and came away without an endorsement. The structural point is sharper: several delegations have argued the **forced-labour tariffs are a pretense for rebuilding the tariff regime that the February Supreme Court ruling hollowed out.** For India the live exposure remains the separate **Section 301 overcapacity investigation**, opened in March 2026 covering **16 economies including India**, which has not yet been imposed and which Greer on Sunday said could credit negotiated tariff caps.

## Europe's Fiscal Crack: French Yields at Their Highest Since 2002

**French government bond yields climbed to their highest levels since 2002** after last week's figures highlighted ballooning public debt, with the **French equity index falling 1%** on the day. The euro sold off, dragging the **dollar close to an 18-month high** on a safe-haven bid, and Spain has been added to Europe's growing list of fiscal trouble spots. In the background, the German government's tax cut on fuel took effect on October 1, easing household energy costs at the margin while adding to the fiscal impulse.

This is a slower-moving variable than a tariff or a rate decision, but it is the one now transmitting to global risk pricing: European sovereign spreads widening into an environment of generational-high yields is the mechanism by which **fiscal tolerance in a high-yield world compresses**, and it is a direct input into the euro-dollar path that India's import bill and IT sector both read.
