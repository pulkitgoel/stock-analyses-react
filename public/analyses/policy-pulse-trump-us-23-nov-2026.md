The president asked for the chief executive back on Sunday night, and the board delivered him by Monday lunchtime.

Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang has been reinstated as president, chief executive and board director, effective immediately, following a unanimous board vote. He had resigned on 12 November — eleven days earlier — after reports of a series of affairs, one with a woman US intelligence suspects of being a Chinese asset. After his departure the stock fell more than 30% and dragged the entire AI complex with it.

The board's own statement is the part worth reading twice. It says it reached the decision "following a series of discussions and meetings with Mr. Huang," that it was satisfied "with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead NVIDIA," and — the operative clause — that "in the process of reaching its decision, the Board received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk."

Trump had written on social media on Sunday night: "I hope the Board of Nvidia rehires Jensen Huang. It would be the best thing for the Company, and also, for the U.S.A." On Monday he said the board had made "the correct and proper decision," that Attorney General Todd Blanche had confirmed the government holds no evidence of unlawful conduct, and that "the United States Government has no reason to believe that Jensen's return to NVIDIA poses any risk to National Security." He called Huang a "Great Patriot and National Treasure," and asked the company to continue its internal investigation and its export-control compliance.

This is a governance event with a market cap attached, and both halves matter. The price reaction was violent: Nvidia traded at $169.37 at 10:30 a.m. ET against Friday's $125.53 close, up 34.9% on the session.

## What the reinstatement does and does not fix

The company has bolted on the guardrails it was missing. Colette Kress, who held the interim chief executive role, stays on as chief financial officer; Harvey Jones remains board chair. Nvidia is creating a National Security and Export Control Committee composed exclusively of independent directors to oversee trade compliance, and is recruiting a chief compliance officer — a newly created position.

None of that closes the legal file. An early-stage SEC inquiry is open, shareholder litigation is running, and Huang is due to appear before Congress on 1 December. The board had said after last week's earnings call that it did not intend to bring him back, and reversed inside a week. An investor buying the 35% move is buying the reinstatement, not a resolution.

There is also a cleaner read-through, and it is the one that matters more for the tape: the selloff of the past two weeks was never really about one executive. It was about whether AI capital spending earns its cost of capital. A board vote does not answer that question. It only removes the idiosyncratic overhang that was sitting on top of it.

## The other Trump policy that landed on the same tape

Two days before the Nvidia reversal, Trump used a Friday afternoon press conference to threaten something with no precedent and no mechanism: that US companies will not be allowed to build AI data centres abroad, and that those that do will face a "tremendous tax" and heavy penalties.

"We're not allowing data centers to go into other countries because China will take all of them," he said. "And we're going to put tremendous penalties on companies that try and do it." He named a proposed data centre in Finland. He also conceded he had not discussed it with his own officials — "I don't know if that's a breaking story, but I haven't even said that to my people, but I'm telling them now."

There is no rate, no start date, no executive order, no agency rule and no bill. A federal tax requires Congress. The Commerce Department already runs chip export controls, which is the lever that actually constrains where American compute can go. Treat this as a threat used as leverage, not as policy — but note what it did to the five most exposed names on a day when the S&P was already down hard: Alphabet -2.70% to $265.29, Microsoft -2.77% to $484.52, Amazon -4.10% to $246.82, Equinix -4.89% to $968.22 and Digital Realty -6.63% to $162.46, the steepest of the group. The data-centre REITs were hit hardest because renting space worldwide is their entire business model, not a side project.

## The executive order that did get signed

Late on Friday, Trump issued an executive order directing federal contractors and subcontractors to consult his administration before responding to inquiries from Congress, and to withhold any communications the White House considers privileged. Companies that fail to comply face termination of their contracts and suspension of future federal work. It applies to all contractors and subcontractors — a universe of tens of thousands of businesses.

The politics are loud and the equity read-through is quiet. For the large defence primes the federal contract is the business, so the compliance cost is real but the revenue is not at risk from this order. Where it bites is at the smaller services layer, where a single agency contract is a large share of revenue and a terminated contract is an event.

The base being bounced off is ugly. On Friday the Dow fell 949.73 points (-1.93%) to 48,147.33, the S&P 265.44 points (-3.70%) to 6,910.39 and the Nasdaq 1,261.47 points (-5.28%) to 22,617.73. The VIX at 35.89 on Friday was its highest since the 2025 "Liberation Day" tariff announcement. At 27.08 it is still elevated. This is a bounce in a high-volatility regime, not a return to calm.

One note on the President's own framing. His Sunday post said the market had lost more than $6 trillion since the Democrats' 3 November midterm win, that the Nasdaq was down 20%, and that losses "will only get worse" unless the party changed course. The Nasdaq's peak-to-Friday-trough drawdown was closer to 20% from its 6 October record, so the number is in the right neighbourhood; the attribution is politics. What is not political is that a Democratic-led Congress now has subpoena power over the investment programme and over federal contracting, and that is a genuine overhang on any company whose revenue depends on discretionary federal awards.
