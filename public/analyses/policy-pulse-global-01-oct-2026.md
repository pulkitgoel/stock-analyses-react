## Milwaukee Day Two: the "Milwaukee Framework" on Steel, and the MFN Principle Put on the Table

**The G20 Trade Ministerial closed its second and final day in Milwaukee on Thursday, and the substantive output is a steel agreement plus an agenda that reaches further into the trading system than any G20 trade meeting in recent memory.**

The United States holds the 2026 G20 presidency, and USTR **Jamieson Greer** framed the meeting's four themes explicitly: **eliminating forced labour in global supply chains, "updating" the Most-Favoured-Nation principle, denouncing the weaponisation of trade in food, and addressing structural excess capacity and production.** In his opening session he argued the MFN principle, "particularly when applied unconditionally, constrains economies' ability to effectively respond to distortive policies and adapt to changing market conditions" — a direct challenge to the cornerstone of the WTO system.

The concrete deliverable was on steel. Participating countries **endorsed what Greer described as the "Milwaukee Framework"**, aimed at **stronger coordination on excess capacity, trade remedies, supply-chain monitoring and transshipment**. It follows the **Global Forum on Steel Excess Capacity** ministerial that opened the meeting on Wednesday, and it sets the template for how the United States intends to handle industrial overcapacity outside the WTO's dispute machinery.

The bilateral traffic around the ministerial is where the market-relevant detail sits:

- **India.** Commerce and Industry Minister **Piyush Goyal met Greer on the sidelines on Thursday** and said on X that they had a **"productive discussion towards early conclusion of a mutually beneficial interim agreement under the India-US Bilateral Trade Agreement."** The two sides announced the framework for the first phase of the BTA in February 2026, and repeated changes in the US tariff landscape have kept negotiations running. Goyal also met **Mexico's Secretary of Economy Marcelo Ebrard** and his Polish counterpart, and is on a week-long US visit running **September 29 to October 5**.
- **Canada.** Canada's trade minister for non-US trade, **Maninder Sidhu, met Goyal on Thursday**, with both sides pushing for a **Canada-India trade deal by year end**. Ottawa cited a **17% increase in non-US trade over the past year, worth $33 billion**. Notably, Canada's US-focused trade minister, **Dominic LeBlanc, did not attend** — and Greer told Fox Business that the US is merely "matching Canadian action," adding that "if the Canadians want a deal, obviously our door is always open," days after Washington implemented import bans on Canadian alcohol, motorcycles and dairy.
- **Brazil.** Trade Minister **Marcio Elias Rosa met Greer on Wednesday** seeking a **"full tariff reduction"** — explicitly including Section 232 steel and aluminium duties, the 12.5% forced-labour tariff and a 25% Section 301 unfair-trade-practice tariff. The two countries agreed to **establish a working group**, with the US to submit a proposal in coming weeks setting out the scope of negotiations.
- **Korea.** Seoul's trade ministry said it has confirmed with the US that the **existing 15% tariff cap will not be exceeded**, regardless of whether the instrument is Section 301 or another authority.

## Section 301's Unfinished Business: an Overcapacity Probe That Names India

**The forced-labour tariffs were argued in court on Wednesday. The tariff authority that has not yet been exercised is the one India should be watching.**

Two distinct USTR tracks are live:

- **The forced-labour tariffs, already in force.** Ten or twelve and a half per cent — depending on whether a government has or has not enacted laws to combat forced labour — on **60 economies spanning 86 countries**, with the 27-member EU counted as a single economy. Those trading partners account for **99.4% of US imports**. Three consolidated lawsuits went before the **Court of International Trade** in New York on Wednesday, brought by small businesses including **spice importer Burlap & Barrel** and **rare watch importer Collective Horology**, with the **Liberty Justice Center** — the firm that won the landmark Supreme Court tariff case — arguing the tariffs are **"blatantly pretextual."** The challengers point to public statements from the President and Treasury Secretary that the new duties were **"doing the same thing"** as the IEEPA tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February, compelling **more than $166 billion in refunds**. The judges raised the possibility of either striking the tariffs down or **remanding the whole matter to USTR to redo the process properly**.
- **The overcapacity investigation, still in the pipeline.** USTR opened **Section 301 investigations into overcapacity and forced labour covering 16 economies in March 2026** — a list that includes **China, South Korea, the EU, Japan and India**. The reported plan is a further set of duties on 16 countries for **"excess manufacturing capacity and production."** That track has **not** been imposed, and it is the live risk to Indian exporters. External Affairs Minister **S. Jaishankar has publicly flagged the Section 301 process** as a concern.

The distinction matters because the two tracks carry different probabilities. The forced-labour tariffs are **in force and legally contested**; the overcapacity duties are **proposed, formally investigated, and squarely aimed at manufacturing exporters including India's**. The G20 language on "structural excess capacity" adopted in Milwaukee is the political scaffolding for the second track, not the first.

## The Bond Market Is Still the Transmission Channel

**The quarter that ended on September 30 delivered the largest US Treasury sell-off since 1994, and it is the single most important variable for equities in both the US and India today.**

- **The US 10-year Treasury yield closed September at 5.304%**, its **highest level since May 2002** and, per Dow Jones Market Data, its **largest quarterly gain since the first quarter of 1994 — a rise of nearly 0.9 percentage points.** The 30-year sat around **5.62%**, its highest since June 2002.
- **Thursday's levels were barely different:** the 10-year held near **5.27-5.28%** after touching **5.34%**, and the 30-year near **5.62%**. Treasuries stabilised only because oil eased into the Asian session that had begun before China's export suspension was digested.
- **The stress is global and concentrated at the long end.** **UK 30-year borrowing costs hit their highest since 1998**, with **NatWest, HSBC, Barclays and Lloyds falling sharply** as the gilt sell-off spread. **Japan's 10-year yield rose five basis points to 3.100%**, and **Australia's 10-year advanced four basis points to 5.39%.**
- **The India transmission is direct and quantifiable.** The rupee fell to **96.3150**, its weakest in two months and its sharpest single-day fall in more than two months; the benchmark 10-year G-sec yield hit a **two-year high**; **foreign investors sold about $1.06 billion of Indian equities on September 30** and roughly **$3.6 billion over five sessions**, taking 2026 outflows to a record **$27.8 billion**; and the Nifty closed out an **eighth consecutive weekly decline, its longest losing run in 25 years.**
- **The offset is the rate-expectations repricing.** After the cooler August PCE print, the market cut the odds of an **October 28 FOMC hike to roughly 35-37% from about 71% a week earlier**, and **Goldman Sachs pushed its forecast for the next hike to December.** That is the one thing that has relieved pressure at the front end — but not at the long end, where term premium, deficits and Treasury supply are the drivers.

## Oil Is Back Above $100 — and the Catalyst Is China, Not Iran

**Brent's December contract traded back above the psychological $100 level on Thursday, up 2.1-2.5% to around $100.09-100.49, after settling at $98.03 on Wednesday.** The November contract expired on Wednesday at **$103.50**. **WTI's November contract traded near $92.48-92.57, up about 2.3%**, from a **$90.42** settlement.

This is a **product-market shock, not a crude-supply shock**, and that distinction matters for how it trades:

- **China's refiners suspended most October fuel exports**, tightening global gasoline and diesel markets that were already strained. Diesel is the binding leg — US diesel is at records and heating oil futures jumped more than 5% in the same session.
- **US–Iran diplomacy stalled.** Secretary of State Marco Rubio reportedly asked an Iranian delegation including Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi to leave the country immediately after talks broke down, reviving the supply-risk premium that had been fading.
- **A US diesel-export restriction remains live in the conversation**, having been floated by the President in September, which would tighten the global product market further.
- **OPEC+ is expected to leave November output targets unchanged at Sunday's online meeting**, keeping the group in a holding pattern while it completes a **DeGolyer & MacNaughton capacity review** that will set the **2027 baselines** determining future quotas and the plans to unwind the remaining cuts. The **Joint Ministerial Monitoring Committee also meets Sunday.** Gulf output is running roughly **5 million barrels per day below February's pre-war level**.
- **The market's own forecast is lower.** The Dallas Fed's survey of oil executives puts **WTI around $88 at the end of 2026**, and the physical workarounds keep accumulating: Saudi Arabia has begun work on the **Duqm pipeline to bypass Hormuz**, the East-West pipeline has restarted, and the **UAE and India are working to expand strategic petroleum reserves and study a subsea gas pipeline.**

## Asia's Tech Relief Rally, and a Month to Forget for Global Equities

**Asian technology shares led a global relief rally on Thursday after Micron's blowout fiscal fourth quarter, in a session where equity futures and bonds were the mirror image of each other.**

- **Japan's Nikkei 225 rose 2.9-3.3%**, having closed Wednesday up **1.94% at 66,753.72**. **South Korea's Kospi rose 1.3-1.7%**, after finishing Wednesday down 0.48% at **6,838.04**. **Australia's S&P/ASX 200** added **0.92% to 8,789.30**.
- **Micron delivered record gross margins again** but warned they would tighten slightly; the stock edged up about 0.4% after hours, and **Nasdaq 100 futures rose about 1-1.2%** while S&P 500 futures gained about 0.6%. The correct reading is that the *sector* read-through was better than the *stock* reaction — the bar was already high.
- **Alphabet rose about 1.7-2.2% pre-market** on the **Gemini 4 Argon** rollout, priced at **$2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output** with a 95% discount on cached input. Google says the model sets a record in real-world software engineering and ties for first in cybersecurity, and it is already being used internally to release hundreds of terabytes of data-centre memory without new hardware.
- **The broader scoreboard is weaker than the day.** The **MSCI All Country World Index recorded its first monthly decline in three months in September**; September 30's US close was **S&P 500 7,651.54 (-0.25%), Dow 50,906.05 (-0.86%), Nasdaq 26,861.06 (+0.24%), Russell 2000 2,796.86 (-0.4%)**; and attention now turns to **Friday's US payrolls report**.

- **Two regulatory items with sector consequences.** The **WTO established a dispute panel on the EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism** at Russia's request, with Moscow arguing CBAM creates significant barriers for covered goods and that free allowance allocation under the EU emissions trading system amounts to an export subsidy. And **Synopsys and Amazon Web Services signed a chip-design licensing deal worth more than $1 billion** — the largest such agreement in the EDA market, and a signal about where AI-chip design demand is being absorbed.
