## The Waiver Is a China Story Too

India got the headlines because Indian goods were the ones facing an imminent 100% duty, but **China is the largest importer of Russian crude** and therefore the largest single exposure under Section 113 of the Graham Act. Friday's [executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/10/implementing-the-lindsey-o-graham-sanctioning-russia-and-iran-act-of-2026/) set the 100% rate on the five largest buyers and then waived every duty under Sections 112 and 113, which means Beijing's tariff exposure is now suspended on exactly the same terms as New Delhi's.

Three things make this more than a diplomatic pause:

- **The waiver is revocable and reviewable.** It runs until the president or the secretary of state decides "further action is appropriate"; Rubio has put a **60-day** review on it, which lands around **mid-December** — after the US midterms and just before the **January 10, 2027 expiry of the US-China tariff truce**.
- **The diesel contradiction is the constraint.** OFAC General License 135 authorises Russian-origin diesel into the US until **April 2027**. Sanctioning countries for buying Russian crude while licensing Russian refined product into American ports is not a position that survives scrutiny, which is why **Shashwat Kumar** of CSIS expects no serious tariff action against China or India before spring.
- **The statutory machinery now exists.** Sections 112 and 113 are law, delegated to the USTR, with monitoring and reporting obligations written into the order. The next administration, or this one after a bad week, does not need new legislation to switch it on.

For European energy majors the read is narrow but real: a waiver that keeps Russian barrels flowing to Asian buyers without triggering secondary tariffs keeps the crude complex supplied and keeps the refined-product arbitrage open, which is a price effect rather than a permission effect.

## France Sends European Equities Lower

European equities sold off hard, and not because of tariffs. **Germany's DAX fell 1.39% to 25,264**, the **CAC 40 fell 1.64% to 7,823**, the **FTSE MIB fell 1.75%**, and the **STOXX Europe 600 fell 1.08%**, with French government bonds also under pressure as violent protests spread across the country and curfews were ordered in multiple cities.

This is political risk pricing, not an earnings event. It matters to Indian and US investors for one reason: France and Germany are the two economies whose fiscal and political stability underwrite the euro, and a French risk premium that widens is a European demand signal for autos, luxury and capital goods.

## Asia Splits: Seoul Up, Tokyo Down

The region did not move as a bloc.

- **KOSPI gained 1.73% to 5,768.01**, with Samsung Electronics up 0.7% and SK hynix down 0.9%.
- **Nikkei 225 fell 0.62% to 64,916.70**, with Tokyo Electron down 2.1% and SoftBank Group — an OpenAI investor — down 3.6%.
- **Taiwan's weighted index fell 0.61%**, **Hang Seng rose 0.51%**, **Shanghai Composite rose 0.39%** and the **ASX 200 gained 0.44%**.

The divergence is a memory-versus-equipment split inside the same AI supply chain: Korean memory names held gains on the DRAM pricing cycle, while Japanese equipment and the SoftBank/OpenAI complex gave back part of last week's move. That is a rotation within AI, not an exit from it — and it is the pattern to watch into the Q3 hyperscaler earnings.
