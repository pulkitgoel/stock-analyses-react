The election is today. The policy that will actually move cash flows arrives after it, and it does not need Congress to pass anything.

That distinction is the whole trade. Wall Street spent the week positioning for a midterm outcome. Goldman Sachs spent this morning telling clients the opposite thing matters — that Trump is likely to *raise* tariffs once the vote is behind him, whoever wins.

## First: the number is just under two percentage points

**Goldman Sachs wrote to clients this morning that it expects Trump to increase tariffs in the event Democrats secure greater Congressional influence.** The note, from economists **Alec Phillips and Abhay Duggirala**, contains the arithmetic that matters:

> "Democrats have indicated they would seek to reverse some of President Trump's tariffs, but we expect this to have a limited effect since the reversals would likely be focused on a few countries (e.g., Canada) or products. We expect President Trump to announce new tariff increases after the election that would more than offset those reductions, likely resulting in a rise in the US effective tariff rate of a little under 2pp."

Read that twice, because it inverts the consensus narrative. The market has been pricing a Democratic win as tariff relief. Goldman is pricing it as **net tariff increase of roughly 2 percentage points on the effective rate** — because the reversals would be narrow and symbolic, while the new increases would be broad.

The reason is mechanical, and Reuters laid it out on 1 November. **Democrats need a net gain of four seats in both chambers** to take the House (currently 218-214) and the Senate (53-47). Polls favour them in the House; the Senate is too close to call. Even a sweep leaves them far short of the two-thirds needed to override a veto. Senator **Chuck Schumer's "End Trump's Tariff Tax Act"** — 28 co-sponsors including Finance Committee ranking member **Ron Wyden** — would scrap **Section 338** of the Tariff Act of 1930 (the Canada tariffs) and **Section 122** of the Trade Act of 1974 (the temporary 10% global rate), and refund all duties collected. It will not become law. What it will become is hearings.

Wyden was explicit about the priority: *"One of the most obvious priorities would be repealing Trump's tariffs on Canada, which are raising prices and harming communities on both sides of the border."* Canada, not China. That is the narrow-reversal Goldman is describing.

**William Reinsch** of CSIS put the legislative reality bluntly: *"Democrats are going to try a lot of things. I just don't think anything's going to get across the finish line because neither party will have a veto-proof majority in any scenario."*

## Second: tariffs are now a revenue line, and revenue lines get defended

ING's **James Smith** framed the post-election logic better than anyone this week, and it is the part that survives the election result:

> "Remember tariffs? After the Supreme Court struck down Trump's emergency tariffs, temporary 10% blanket rates ultimately gave way to a more permanent structure under Section 301. The issue has since slipped remarkably far down the agenda."

He then makes the connection most analysts are missing. With fiscal deficits at roughly **6% of GDP** against a stated goal of 3%, and any deficit reduction likely to bog down in divided government, the pressure moves *toward* trade policy as a source of receipts — not away from it.

The live vehicle is already in motion. Reuters reports a **forthcoming investigation into excess industrial capacity across 16 major trading partners**, running in parallel with a **USTR Section 301 excess-capacity probe into India**. That is the mechanism by which a post-election increase would arrive: not a new emergency declaration, but a statutory finding that carries no sunset.

There is a counterweight worth keeping in view, because it shows the threats do not always convert. **On 28 October Trump said imported robotics pose national security risks and then did not impose tariffs on them.** A president who declines a ready-made action one week is a president whose next action is a choice, not a certainty.

## Third: oil fell 3% while the US was bombing Iran

The more interesting price action today is crude, because the gap between the headline and the tape is unusually wide.

**Brent was down 3% at $101.54 and WTI down 2.3% at $94.23** at the time of writing, a second consecutive session of declines, after Monday's **7.63% collapse** that took Brent to a $104.77 settle with a $103.28 session low.

The trigger for the fall was diplomatic. Trump told reporters Tehran wants *"to make a deal and that could happen."* Vice President **JD Vance** told CNN: *"The president and I both feel really good about the [prospect of potentially coming] to a negotiated settlement."* Secretary of State **Marco Rubio** said on Sunday that Trump would prefer to resolve the conflict diplomatically rather than by force.

The offsetting fact, reported the same day: **the US attacked Iran's Qeshm Island on Monday**, according to US officials speaking to the Wall Street Journal, and has been conducting **"limited strikes"** against the country for weeks. The campaign has been kept deliberately low-profile to avoid signalling a major escalation. Commercial shipping continues to be attacked — **at least four vessel attacks since the start of November** in the Persian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman, per the UK Maritime Trade Operations Centre.

So the market is removing a war premium from a war that is still being fought, on the strength of statements rather than a signed agreement. That is a defensible trade — the premium was for a *future* escalation — but it is a probability being repriced, not a risk being retired. Oil gained more than 10% in October; a 7.6% single-day giveback returns it to roughly where the month began.

## Fourth: the funding cliff nobody is pricing

One date on the calendar is being almost completely ignored and it lands five weeks out. **Federal government funding runs out on 11 December.** Trump signed the Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act on 2 September, funding agencies through a 72-day window that expires that day, and a continuing resolution needs bipartisan support to prevent a shutdown.

Deutsche Bank's read is that Republicans will attempt a party-line reconciliation bill in the coming weeks that could add substantially to defence spending and address the debt limit — but reconciliation is exactly the kind of manoeuvre that a divided Congress makes harder, not easier.

## On the disclosure front: nothing new, and I am not going to invent an angle

**There is no new presidential financial disclosure today.** The most recent remains the filing covering August-period transactions, which was released on 8 October: **517 transactions, purchases of at least $44.2 million and sales of at least $30.1 million**, including up to **$25 million of Meta** and up to **$5 million of SpaceX debt** purchased two days before the space-transportation memorandum.

Publishing a fresh read on a filing that is now more than three weeks old would be worse than saying there is nothing. The pattern to watch for is the *next* filing, and specifically whether any purchase lands in the days before a tariff action — that was the tell in July, when the trades preceded the tariff pause, and it is the only thing in these disclosures that has ever been actionable.
