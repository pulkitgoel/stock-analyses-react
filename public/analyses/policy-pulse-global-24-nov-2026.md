## The list that matters more than the bounce

The document to watch this week is not Asian price action. It is the Section 301 structural excess-capacity determination that USTR is reportedly preparing to publish, possibly this week.

The investigation was opened on 11 March 2026 against 16 economies — China, the EU, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan and India — which together supplied close to $2.4 trillion of US goods imports last year, just over 70% of the total. The sectors in scope are steel, aluminium, vehicles, batteries, chemicals, electronics, machinery, semiconductors, solar modules, ships and transport equipment.

The reporting from the Wall Street Journal, confirmed in substance by Indian, Korean and Japanese coverage, says the rates will be tiered. Japan, Korea and the EU, which have signed agreements, land at 15% or below. India and Vietnam are expected to be higher. China is expected to be highest. Steel, aluminium and automobiles are expected to be excluded because Section 232 already covers them. The new duties would stack on top of the 10%–12.5% forced-labour tariffs that took effect on 24 July, and officials are reportedly drafting a ratchet allowing further increases if a country's overcapacity worsens.

For India specifically, this is the binding constraint on a trade deal that has been described as all but finished since September. The interim framework announced on 7 February would have cut the reciprocal tariff on Indian goods from 25% to 18% and withdrawn a further 25% penalty tied to Russian crude purchases. The Supreme Court's February ruling against the IEEPA tariffs erased the legal basis under which that framework was negotiated. Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agrawal said on 18 November that the deal cannot be signed until the new tariff architecture exists and the investigations are resolved: "Until the tariff architecture comes back in place, signing of a deal, I think will take some time."

So the sequence for India is: determination first, then rates, then a bilateral agreement negotiated against those rates. Not the other way round. Any expectation of a near-term deal that removes tariff uncertainty is, on the current evidence, a 2027 expectation.

## What a goods duty touches in India, and what it does not

This is the part that is usually blurred, and it is the most useful thing to get right.

A Section 301 duty is an import duty on goods. It does not reach services. That means the entire Indian IT services complex — the country's largest single export category to the United States — is outside the instrument, no matter what rate India is assigned. Whatever else happens to the tariff schedule in the next six weeks, TCS, Infosys and HCLTech face no direct duty line from it. Their exposure runs the other way, through US client capex and through the rupee.

The exposure sits in goods: textiles and apparel, chemicals and speciality chemicals, auto components, electronics assembled for export, solar modules, shrimp and marine products, and the labour-intensive categories that the forced-labour action already covers.

There is a second-order effect worth flagging because it is counter-intuitive: a higher tariff on Indian goods makes the rupee weaker, and a weaker rupee improves the landed competitiveness of every Indian exporter that is not tariffed and raises the reported revenue of every IT contract priced in dollars. That is not a reason to own the sector. It is a reason not to treat "India gets a higher rate" as uniformly negative for Indian equities.
