## Trump Waives the Graham Act Hours Before It Was Due to Bite

The executive order is titled [Implementing the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/10/implementing-the-lindsey-o-graham-sanctioning-russia-and-iran-act-of-2026/) and it is dated **October 16, 2026**. It does two opposite things in the same document.

First it sets the rates:

- **Section 2(a)** — the rate of duty on goods from the **Russian Federation rises 500% ad valorem**, subject to the Act's exceptions.
- **Section 2(b)** — goods from the **five largest importers of Russian-origin crude oil and natural gas** face a **100% ad valorem** increase, but only where the country **knowingly makes new purchases on or after October 18, 2026**. Purchases made before that date do not count.
- **Section 2(c)** — a further **100%** on goods from the **top five facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion**, with the USTR tasked to monitor and report which countries qualify.
- **Section 2(d)** — all of it stacks on top of existing duties, including AD/CVD and Sections 122, 201, 301 and 232.

Then **Section 4** waives the lot. Under **Section 115(a)** of the Act the president waived the duties provided for in Sections 112 and 113 and in the order itself, plus the sanctions in Sections 102(a), 102(d), 102(e), 103 through 111 and 117, after submitting to Congress the written certification that the waiver is in the national interest and a report explaining the basis. The waivers "remain in effect until such time as the President or the Secretary of State ... determine that further action is appropriate."

That last sentence is the whole trade policy in one clause: the rates are now written in statute and delegated to the USTR, and the only thing standing between them and a customs bill is a revocable piece of paper.

**Why now, and what it costs him.** Secretary of State **Marco Rubio** said on Saturday that Trump would revisit the decision in **60 days**, after officials assess "what imposing both sanctions and tariffs will mean to the global economy". Trump's own explanation, in a Sunday Truth Social post, was that he was worried about driving Russia away from the negotiating table and was displeased with Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries. The political cost was immediate: **Senator John Cornyn**, leaving Congress in January, posted that "Lindsey would be appalled"; **Senator Ted Cruz** called it "a mistake" on Fox News; Ways and Means ranking member **Richard Neal** said the president had surrendered to Putin; and **Volodymyr Zelenskyy** told Meet the Press on Sunday that the move was "taking Putin's side" and a "historic mistake".

The unstated reason is the calendar. Imposing triple-digit tariffs on the five largest buyers of Russian energy three weeks before the **November 3 midterms** would have been a price shock in an election being fought on affordability. **Daniel Mullaney**, a former assistant USTR, put the durable function plainly: the tariff powers "are more likely to be used as leverage than an actual sanctions tool."

The contradiction underneath it is the diesel licence. **OFAC General License 135**, issued October 9, authorises Russian-origin diesel into the United States until **April 2027**. Waiving secondary tariffs on countries that buy Russian crude, while licensing Russian refined product into US ports, is a combination that would have been difficult to defend at a WTO or in a press briefing. **Shashwat Kumar** of CSIS told Politico he doubts the administration seriously considers tariffs on China and India before spring, and that the diesel deal expiring in April is precisely when the hypocrisy argument weakens.

**The market read is a supply-risk discount coming out of crude, not a tariff coming out of goods.** Brent traded around **$108.30, down about 1.4%**, and WTI around **$100.75, down 0.65%** in the US morning; ICIS attributed the fall directly to the waiver tempering supply worries. US equities opened higher with the Nasdaq up 1.5% at 11:11 am ET, the 10-year at 5.131% and the VIX down 11%.

## The Aluminium Fight Moves Into the USMCA Talks

The metals tariffs are now the live negotiating file, and the producer lobby is refusing to let them be traded away. **Century Aluminum** president and CEO **Jesse Gary** told Politico on Saturday that the administration should preserve Section 232 protections in the USMCA review: "when you look at frankly both the steel and aluminum tariffs, the program has been extremely effective. It's driving exactly the type of reshoring that we want ... the programs are effective as they are, and they should stay as they are."

Century operates smelters in Kentucky and South Carolina and is among the manufacturers backing the **50% metals tariffs**. Its argument is aimed squarely at the Wall Street Journal report that the US-Mexico tariff deal has stalled because **Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick** opposes cutting the **25% tariff on Mexican autos**, the **50% steel and aluminium levies**, and the duties on derivative products made from those metals.

Gary also expects metals protection to survive a Democratic takeover of Congress, calling reshoring "one of the true bipartisan issues" and noting the Biden administration kept most of the first-term metals tariffs.

The practical consequence is that the metals levies have been reclassified from negotiating chip to industrial policy. Every North American manufacturer that had been modelling relief now has to model the current landed cost persisting through the next window — and every domestic smelter gets a protected price floor for longer.

## The Pentagon Starts Buying Pharmaceutical Supply Chains

On **Saturday** the **Department of War's Office of Strategic Capital** committed to an **$80 million conditional loan to Antheia**, a biomanufacturer that has been relying on overseas partners for the drug precursors and key starting materials behind its products. The financing is specifically to stand up US manufacturing of those inputs.

The strategic logic is in the numbers Politico cited: more than **90% of the raw materials required to make amoxicillin are under Chinese control**, as are roughly **70% of the materials used in acetaminophen**, according to Council on Foreign Relations analysis. **Emil Michael**, undersecretary of defence for research and engineering, framed it as one of the Department of War's **six critical technology areas**.

This is the third leg of the pharma-onshoring policy stack, alongside the Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs that went live on **July 31** and the 20% onshoring incentive for companies moving production to the United States. It is also the first time the defence capital window — previously used for critical minerals — has been pointed at drug precursors.

## Chips Rebound Off the Worst Week Since June

Friday was ugly: the **Nasdaq Composite closed at 26,140.03, down 2.14%**, the **S&P 500 at 7,659.44, down 1.09%** and the **Dow at 52,002.41, down 0.34%**, with the VIX up 7% to 18.58 — the worst week for the Nasdaq since June, driven by the AI-debt story and oil.

Monday reversed it. At **11:11 am ET** the Nasdaq was **26,537.71, up 1.52%**, the S&P 500 **7,714.99, up 0.73%**, the Dow **52,039.55, up 0.07%**, the Russell 2000 up 0.37%, and the **VIX down 11% to 17.79**. Two things did the work: **JPMorgan upgraded Intel to Neutral from Underweight**, citing better execution and competitiveness in data-centre compute, and crude and yields both eased.

- **Intel rose about 4%**, after falling more than 14% last week — the upgrade lands on a much lower base, and the stock is still up roughly 140% in 2026.

- **AMD rose about 4%**, and the iShares Semiconductor ETF was up about 3%.
- The 10-year Treasury yield held near **5.13%**, so this was an oil-and-positioning rally rather than a rates rally.
